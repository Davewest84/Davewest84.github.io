Claude and ChatGPT Connectors: Link Your Favorite Tools to AI

Jeremy Caplan, writing on his Wonder Tools newsletter, argues that AI assistants have quietly graduated from being answer machines into something closer to a digital command centre. The mechanism is the Model Context Protocol, or MCP, which lets you plug your everyday software into Claude or ChatGPT so the assistant can actually reach into your email, calendar, notes and files rather than just talking about them. Caplan compares MCP to the arrival of USB: before USB, every device needed its own proprietary cable; MCP is the standard connector that does away with all of that, letting tools, sites and apps link to the AI platforms in a uniform way. Setup needs no coding — you pick a service, log in, and approve the permissions, and you can restrict those to "view only" if you would rather the assistant could not change anything.

The pay-off is that you can issue genuinely compound requests. His flagship example is asking the assistant to add four prep sessions to open morning slots on his calendar next week for the classes he is teaching in the autumn, and to paste relevant excerpts from his meeting summaries into each event description — a single instruction that touches calendar, teaching schedule and notes at once. He lists a long roster of connectable tools, including Superhuman and Gmail for mail, Granola for meeting transcription, Readwise, Raindrop and Sublime for saved highlights, Google Drive and Calendar, time trackers like Rize, project tools like Airtable, Asana and ClickUp, and creative apps from Adobe and Canva.

Caplan is careful to flag the risks. He warns against connecting sensitive financial accounts, points to the danger of prompt injection, and notes the exposure if your phone is stolen. His sharpest cautionary tale is a Cursor AI incident that deleted a company's database and its backup. His rule of thumb: don't give AI systems autonomous power to send emails, submit forms or modify files without a human reviewing first. He also notes that Google's Gemini does not yet make MCP connections easy.

Five Useful AI Tactics to Sharpen Your Thinking Every Day

In a companion piece, Caplan makes the case that the best use of these tools is to enhance your own cognition, not replace it. He offers five tactics. The first, and the one he leads with, is to tell the AI to challenge you rather than flatter you: present it with a decision or a plan and ask it to play devil's advocate, to name your blind spots, the risks you have overlooked, and the alternative views you have not considered. He suggests casting the assistant in specific roles — coach, teacher, family member, competitor, audience member — each of which surfaces different objections. He notes that Microsoft Copilot now has a "Team of Advisors" feature that supplies several expert perspectives at once.

His second tactic he calls thinking out loud, or "bionic dictation". Rather than asking the AI to write for him, he talks in an unstructured stream of consciousness and lets voice tools such as Letterly, Wispr Flow, Lispr or MacWhisper capture it and reshape it into an outline. His own phrase is worth quoting: he is not asking the voice AI to write something for him, or to think for him, but using it "like a digital bucket" — catch raw thought, then organise it.

The third tactic is to run deep research before any significant decision, using the dedicated research modes now built into ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Copilot and Claude, which can produce citation-rich reports in minutes; detailed queries yield far better results. The fourth is to learn something genuinely new using structured study modes such as ChatGPT's Study and Learn or Gemini's Guided Learning to build a personalised curriculum. The fifth is to build yourself a learning dashboard using tools like Claude Artifacts, Gemini Canvas, Lovable or Bolt, to track progress on reading goals, fitness or a writing project. The through line across all five: the machine is a sparring partner and a scaffold, not a substitute for doing the thinking yourself.
