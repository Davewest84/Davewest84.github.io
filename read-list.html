Why Googlebook could finally deliver the perfect 2-in-1 convertible experience

This is a piece from Chrome Unboxed arguing that Google's forthcoming Googlebook laptops, expected later this autumn, could at last solve a problem that has dogged convertible laptops for more than a decade. The writer is openly enthusiastic but keeps some hesitation about how the desktop side will feel. The core of the argument is architectural. Where today's Chromebooks run on a legacy ChromeOS built on Linux underpinnings, the Googlebook will ship with a full Android technology stack underneath. And the author thinks that shift matters most for the traditional 2-in-1 convertible, the kind of laptop with a 360-degree hinge that folds flat so you can flip the keyboard behind the screen and use it as a tablet.

The promise of those convertibles has always been appealing. Use it as a proper laptop at your desk, then fold it round to read a comic, watch a film, or browse the web on the sofa without having to find somewhere to put the keyboard. But the writer is blunt that ChromeOS tablet mode has always been clunky. Because the operating system was built around a mouse and keyboard desktop, the touch gestures, the animations and the interface scaling always felt like an afterthought bolted onto a desktop layout. Stuttering animations, virtual keyboard quirks and a half-baked interface meant he simply stopped using tablet mode on his convertibles altogether.

Putting Android at the core, he argues, changes the equation. Instead of forcing a desktop-first system to behave like a touch-first one, a Googlebook folded into tablet shape should inherit the same fluid gestures, navigation and interface that Google already does well on devices like the Pixel Tablet. He is careful to note this has not been a Google-only failing. Windows convertibles are strong productivity machines that become unoptimised headaches the moment you treat them as tablets, and Apple has never even put a touchscreen in a MacBook. His bet is that the Android foundation could make Googlebook the first platform to nail both sides, a polished desktop workspace with the keyboard out and a genuine premium tablet the moment you fold it away. He expects premium manufacturers to show refined convertibles at the autumn launch, while acknowledging Google still has to stick the landing.
