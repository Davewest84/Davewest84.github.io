My week with new Googlebooks: the good, the bad, and the promising (ZDNET)

Kyle Kucharski, a senior editor at ZDNET, spent a week using the new Asus Googlebook 14 as his main laptop, and also tried the Dell XPS Googlebook, after going hands on with all five launch models from Asus, Dell, Acer, HP and Lenovo. His verdict is that Google has an exciting new device category on its hands, but the first generation is hit and miss.

GooglebookOS feels to him like ChromeOS evolved, with the Android phone placed front and centre, borrowing openly from the seamless iPhone and Mac integration Apple has spent years refining. On paper you get quick sharing of files, app casting from phone to laptop, picking up where you left off, and the whole Play Store library. In practice, pairing was fiddly even for someone who sets up hundreds of devices a year. He writes: "If it was that touchy for me, I don't even want to think about how far my mom would get." His tip is to set up the phone first and let the laptop pull its data over by QR code, but even then Cross-Device Services was switched off by default on his Pixel 11 Pro, the devices had to be put into a device group, and the laptop still sometimes failed to see a phone sitting right next to it. Quick Share worked better from phone to laptop than the reverse, and he found himself missing AirDrop.

Android apps all ran but with uneven polish. Most are tablet versions not built for keyboard and mouse. Prime Video looked superb on the 3K OLED screen, Spotify claimed to be desktop optimised but was really the mobile app, and the machine hard crashed when he plugged in a Sony camera during a deadline, sending him back to his Windows PC.

On hardware, all five sit above Chromebook Plus territory, with Intel Core Ultra 5 or 7 chips or Qualcomm's Snapdragon X Elite. The Asus weighs about 2.1 pounds and has a tactile, fingerprint resistant nano ceramic finish, but there is some chassis flex and an edge to edge touchpad that causes misclicks. The Dell struck him as the most premium build. His bigger complaint is that the interface is busier than ChromeOS, with Gemini and AI prompts at every turn, including a demand to download AI models just to send a selfie. Still, he gives it grace as a genuine first generation product, and says there is a ton of promise if Google simplifies setup and polishes the interface over the coming months.

What Scotland and Greater Manchester are learning about prevention (King's Fund blog, Ruth Robertson)

This King's Fund blog, published on the seventh of October by senior fellow Ruth Robertson, draws out three lessons from the first two sessions of a new learning partnership between Public Health Scotland and the Greater Manchester Combined Authority, which the Fund is facilitating. Both places are attempting system wide shifts of public services towards prevention. The partnership has settled on four priorities: increasing the share of public spending that goes on prevention, evaluating complex whole system change, building data that works across organisational boundaries, and leading transformation at scale in an unstable policy environment.

The first lesson is to define what counts as prevention, with definitions that are, in a phrase Robertson liked, "as narrow as possible and as broad as necessary". The Scottish government has committed to tracking a rising share of spending on prevention, and this summer began a national pilot mapping spending across two fifths of its budget to decide what qualifies. Greater Manchester is agreeing a definition alongside a theory of change and logic model, as the backbone for future evaluation. In both cases, she says, the process of agreeing the definition is doing as much work as the definition itself.

The second lesson is that technical and relational work have to go together. Her examples are Clackmannanshire, where pooled budgets are now partly decided by people with lived experience through the Family Wellbeing Partnership, and Wigan, which has spent more than a decade on a relational, strengths based model and this year had its adult social care rated outstanding by the Care Quality Commission. She cites local analysis showing Wigan is among the lowest spenders on long term care in the North West and has nine per cent fewer people in long term care than its population would predict. New funding models and governance only stick, she argues, when paired with sustained work on culture and sharing power.

The third lesson, which she calls the Gogglebox effect, is the value of booking a second, smaller conversation straight after each session. In this case it surfaced the Structural Prevention Test: something only counts as preventive if it meets at least two of three criteria, namely shifting power to communities and individuals, building capability rather than managing dependence, and challenging silos rather than reinforcing them. One participant's line stuck with her: "investing in prevention can feel like investing in uncertainty." She closes by noting that with the devolution white paper still awaited, English front runners like Greater Manchester are increasingly looking outside England for inspiration.

Population health in Greater Manchester: the journey so far (King's Fund report, Oct 2024)

This is an older piece, a King's Fund report from October 2024 by Joni Jabbal and David Buck, saved alongside the prevention blog. It tells the story of how Greater Manchester, the poster child for English devolution, became the leading light for improving population health at scale, and asks whether it is working.

The Fund was first invited in during 2019 to assess the approach. After Covid it was asked back to, in its words, hold up a mirror. The team reviewed the literature and the region's own plans, ran three detailed case studies in Bury, Stockport and Manchester between January 2023 and February 2024, and tested the findings in two workshops with more than forty senior leaders from statutory and voluntary bodies.

The headline finding is that the focus on population health is starting to pay off, with greater improvement than would be expected on key measures of health and health inequalities. The authors frame population health around four pillars: the wider determinants such as housing, jobs and environment; health behaviours; coordinated access to health and care services; and the communities and relationships that make people resilient. Greater Manchester's advantage, they argue, has been a clear and stable vision held through Covid and through the transition from the Health and Social Care Partnership to a formal integrated care system, backed by analytical capacity, an academy model for skills, strong networks of directors of population health, and an openness to outside scrutiny. In each of the three boroughs they found economic and health goals aligned, clear governance at the right level, and an emphasis on working with communities. But it has not been plain sailing. Severe financial pressure is squeezing the bandwidth for long term goals, and the move to a formal ICS was disruptive.

The recommendations are pointed. Government should deliver its health mission through all four pillars, not the NHS alone, and make sure the growth mission does not widen inequalities. The Department of Health and Social Care and NHS England should restate population health as a core ICS goal, align accountability and incentives to it, and, in a line worth noting given the ICB reorganisation since, "resist the urge to reorganise system footprints, since long-term goals such as population health require constancy and stability." Other systems are told to build a widely owned vision, be clear about system versus local roles, keep building capability, and treat health and economic goals as intertwined and co-dependent.

Labour's last chance? The people shaping Andy Burnham's grand plans for Britain (Guardian long read, Hettie O'Brien)

Hettie O'Brien's long read, published on the twenty fourth of September, maps the small network of thinkers trying to shape Andy Burnham's government, and asks whether a prime minister who arrived without a leadership contest or a manifesto is really prepared to end forty years of neoliberalism, as he says he wants to.

It opens at a Compass event at the Ministry of Sound in May 2025, where Burnham's closing speech struck Neal Lawson as "the speech of someone who looked and sounded like they wanted to be prime minister". Economists at the Joseph Rowntree Foundation, the New Economics Foundation and IPPR then worked to persuade him that growth would arrive too late and he should intervene directly on the cost of living, timing their rent control research to Josh Simons standing down in Makerfield. In office Burnham has cut energy bills and bus fares and made promises on homelessness, council housing and social care. But O'Brien notes he has already watered down the housing pledge, dropped a donations cap, and swapped "public ownership" for the vaguer "public control". Karel Williams calls him "the master of vibes", a screen onto which Labour factions project their hopes.

She identifies three camps. The first, around Miatta Fahnbulleh and Ed Miliband, draws on Mathew Lawrence of Common Wealth, whose Productive State paper argues life's essentials should sit in publicly run corporations. The second, around Louise Haigh, her adviser Nick Williams and Simons, with the Centre for British Progress and the Good Growth Foundation, blames eroded state capacity and over-regulation rather than privatisation. The third and most powerful are the communitarians inside No 10: chief of staff James Purnell, policy unit head Graeme Cooke and new adviser Marc Stears, who care more about renewing faith in politics through devolution than about who owns what.

The piece then tests Manchesterism against the record: the Bee bus network as public control rather than ownership, the Live Well hubs, and whether a model that worked for buses can govern water. Burnham's own diagnosis, from a 2025 speech, is quoted: "We now have a hollowed-out local state combined with soulless, top-down public services. That is becoming, in my view, a toxic combination." The devolution statement Rewiring the State was, for Jack Shaw, "like Christmas for devo wonks", and No 10 North in Manchester may prove a gimmick or his most consequential policy.

The sceptics get space too. Yuan Yang concedes Reform mayors would use the same powers for light touch regulation. Patrick Diamond warns Labour fears incompetence elsewhere boomeranging on the centre. Ben Houchen has already said he would use devolved income tax for rebates. Diane Coyle says growth in every postcode is overambitious because "some places will mess it up". Rochdale's Atom Valley promises twenty thousand jobs, but Williams points to Ambition North Wales, which after five years has delivered sixty three full time roles against a target of four thousand two hundred. O'Brien concludes the rhetoric cannot be delivered in what remains of this parliament, but that building a more visible, democratic, place based state has to start now, and may mean Britain relearning how to govern itself.
