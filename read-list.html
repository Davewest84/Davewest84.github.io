Oura Ring 5 review: a stunning generational leap for smart rings — The Guardian

The Guardian's consumer technology editor Samuel Gibbs calls the new Oura Ring 5 a generational leap for smart rings, and the headline verdict is that it sets a very high new bar. The big change is size. The ring is 40% smaller in volume than its predecessor, cutting the width to just over 6mm, the thickness to a little over 2mm, and the weight to a featherlight 2g. Gibbs says the numbers don't do it justice, and that in the flesh it is now so close to a standard wedding band that you can genuinely forget you're wearing it — "if you didn't know it was an Oura, you wouldn't guess it was a piece of technology on your finger." It no longer catches on pockets or digs in when you carry shopping or lift weights, though it's also less of a conversation starter as a result.

Despite shrinking, battery life has improved to roughly a week between charges, from six days on the smallest size up to nine on the largest. The titanium ring comes in six finishes with a harder, more scratch-resistant coating, and eight sizes, with a free sizing kit recommended before you buy. It still tracks more than 50 metrics across sleep, activity, readiness, stress, resilience, heart and women's health.

Gibbs is clear that the hardware is only half the story. The real strength is the app and data analysis, which he rates as best in class — comprehensive but intelligible without a degree in data science, and stronger than most health-focused smartwatches. He singles out the women's health tracking as market-leading, and praises the AI chatbot Advisor, built only around your own data, as surprisingly useful for spotting long-term trends.

The catches are cost and repairability. The ring starts at £399 and requires a £5.99-a-month subscription to unlock anything beyond basic live stats. Running and workout tracking is weak without your phone. Crucially, the ring can't be repaired and the battery can't be replaced, making it effectively disposable once the battery wears out — costing it a star. Even so, Gibbs concludes it is one of the very best health-tracking wearables available in any form.

Why the Googlebook will be the perfect middle ground between MacBook and Windows laptops — Chrome Unboxed

This piece argues that Google's forthcoming Googlebook, due in autumn 2026, lands in an ideal sweet spot between Apple's tightly controlled vertical integration and the fragmented openness of Windows. The idea is that Google offers hardware diversity without giving up software optimisation or a consistent user experience. Launch partners are said to include Acer, Asus, HP, Lenovo and Dell, and manufacturers will be free to design their own form factors — clamshells, convertibles, detachables — each with its own design language.

The crucial constraint is that Google enforces strict hardware and software specifications for any device carrying the Googlebook badge. Every machine runs a unified native Android tech stack with consistent features, including a Gemini-powered Magic Pointer and an interactive Glow Bar, and the ecosystem is built for seamless integration between Android phones and laptops. The author quotes Google VP John Maletis: "We want that consistent look and feel, but we also want our partners to be able to shine as well."

The conclusion is that by keeping centralised quality control while allowing manufacturers room to innovate, the Googlebook sidesteps the weaknesses of both rivals. Developers get a single, unfragmented framework to target, and the shared foundation should finally fix the Bluetooth handoff problems that plague current ChromeOS devices, giving Android users a laptop companion that works as smoothly as Apple's ecosystem. The author sums the approach up neatly as "variation with guardrails."
