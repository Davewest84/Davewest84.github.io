'Gemini built in' coming to 3rd-party smart speakers in 2026

Google has launched a programme called Gemini built in, designed to put its AI assistant inside smart speakers, cameras and other devices made by other companies during 2026. Rather than building all the hardware itself, Google is handing manufacturers turnkey reference designs they can adapt. The new addition for this year is a Speaker Reference Design, which Google says lets partners build "high-fidelity speakers that support the full Gemini voice experience" and act as "the command center for the home". That sits alongside an existing Camera Reference Design for Gemini-capable cameras.

The first partners are already emerging. Walmart's Onn brand is among them, and an Onn-branded Gemini smart speaker has already leaked. AT&T is going further, folding Google Home Premium and Gemini into its Connected Life home security service, including the intelligent Nest Cam features. For developers, the programme opens up smart home tools such as Ask Home and Nest Cam's scene recognition, and it lets carriers and internet providers build Gemini features directly into their own apps.

The strategic read: Google wants Gemini everywhere in the home, and is willing to let other brands carry the hardware to get there. There's no firm launch date yet for Google's own Home Speaker, but the direction of travel is clear — the assistant, not the box it lives in, is the product Google cares about.

Google I/O showed how the path for AI-driven science is shifting

This piece argues that something fundamental is changing in how the big labs approach scientific discovery. For years the standout results came from specialised, purpose-built tools — AlphaFold, which won DeepMind scientists a Nobel Prize and is now used by more than three million researchers, or WeatherNext, Google's forecasting model that gave advance warning of Hurricane Melissa in Jamaica. The shift now under way is towards general-purpose agentic systems: large language models that can plan and carry out research with far less human direction. Google has bundled this work into a package called Gemini for Science, bringing together its AI Co-Scientist and a system called AlphaEvolve.

The tone from the top has become strikingly bold. Demis Hassabis, who runs Google DeepMind, said the company is "currently standing in the foothills of the singularity", and suggested that within a decade these systems could shift from tools to genuine collaborators. Pushmeet Kohli put it more directly: "We are moving toward AI that doesn't just facilitate science but begins to do science."

There are concrete signs this is more than rhetoric. A general-purpose reasoning model from OpenAI recently disproved an important mathematics conjecture, and a Stanford geneticist described using the AI Co-Scientist as "consulting the oracle of Delphi". Perhaps the most telling detail: John Jumper, the Nobel laureate behind AlphaFold, has reportedly been reassigned to AI coding work. The conclusion is that even as the specialised tools keep delivering, Google is visibly shifting resources and public positioning toward autonomous systems — and we may be watching the early stage of a change that reshapes how science itself gets done.

Google's AI Studio now lets anyone build Android apps in minutes

At its developer conference on 19 May, Google announced that its web-based AI Studio can now generate native Android apps from a plain description, turning what used to take weeks into a matter of minutes. The apps it produces are real, using the Kotlin language and Google's Jetpack Compose toolkit rather than throwaway prototypes. You can tap into phone hardware such as GPS, Bluetooth and NFC, preview the result in an Android emulator built into the browser, and install it on your own phone over a USB cable using the Android Debug Bridge. There's also an export path to GitHub and a route into the Google Play Console for internal testing.

For now this is pitched at personal use — building a utility for yourself or a small AI-powered app — with sharing to family and friends still on the roadmap, along with deeper Firebase integration for databases and sign-in. Google paired the announcement with a discovery feature called Ask Play, an AI overlay that lets you find apps in the Play Store through natural language, with Gemini surfacing relevant apps mid-conversation.

The competitive context is hard to miss: this pushes Google directly up against the wave of AI coding tools such as Cursor, Replit, Lovable and Claude Code, while lowering the barrier to Android development for people who've never written a line of code. The promise is democratisation; the open question is how good the generated apps actually are once you move beyond the simplest cases.
