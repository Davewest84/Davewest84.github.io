What a time to be alive (Neil O'Brien's Substack)

O'Brien opens in a mood of wonder. Quoting Paul Simon's line about the days of miracle and wonder, he says that every week or two something happens that makes him feel we are finally living in the future. He runs through the fields being upended: OpenAI releasing solutions to previously unsolved maths problems, AI detecting more cancers and outperforming radiologists, AI-designed drugs moving into late-phase trials, nine new antibiotic candidates designed after screening more than seventy billion molecules, and GitHub reporting that AI agents now start over forty per cent of pull requests, up from seventeen per cent in March. He cites a METR report suggesting Anthropic is improving its models on well-specified tasks at around twice the rate it managed between March 2024 and October 2025.

His real argument is that, below the debate about existential risk, there is too little discussion of the big, boring things Britain needs to do to prepare. He picks three.

The first is security. AI makes finding and exploiting vulnerabilities radically cheaper, and he describes October as Black October, with US banks detecting unauthorised AI agents in customer accounts. He notes that in June the US Commerce Department ordered Anthropic to suspend foreign access to its newest models for a couple of weeks, which he says shows why staying close to the frontier matters: if your defensive model is six months behind, the gap is huge. Of most interest to an NHS audience, he writes: "I dread waking up one day soon to find some large bit of the NHS has been shut down by someone exploiting one of its ancient systems." He suggests a drive to patch what he calls dire NHS IT, and points to a summer hackathon that found four hundred and seven vulnerabilities in nine government bodies. He adds biosecurity, citing a Microsoft red-team exercise in which between thirty and seventy per cent of AI-redesigned toxin variants slipped past DNA suppliers' screening software.

The second is macroeconomics. He thinks a dotcom-style AI crash is plausible at some point, flagging the circular flows between Nvidia and its customers, and argues this strengthens the case for sorting out the public finances, while also watching for mispriced assets and investment that could move to Britain.

The third is compute. Britain has a twentieth of America's computing power, and he cites Rhinegroup work suggesting that gap could reach twenty-five times by 2030. He sees an opening for post-industrial places with big sites and existing grid connections, but finds the government's new Data Centre Covenant underwhelming: long on what operators must do, short on what government will do.

He closes by calling the piece a conversation starter rather than a programme, and asks readers how UK policy makers should respond.
