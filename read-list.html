No.10 North: the inside story of the first 100 days (Civil Service World)

This is a first-person account by Sam Lister, director general of No.10 North, the Manchester outpost Andy Burnham set up within hours of becoming prime minister in late July. It first appeared in the Heywood Quarterly. A handful of staff turned up at Heron House, a nondescript red-brick building opposite Manchester Town Hall, and by the Friday of that first week the institution had been formally launched. Burnham told the thirty or so staff it was, quote, "the best day of my life".

Lister's showcase example is the West Midlands buses. National Express had become notorious for so-called ghost buses, cut routes and fines for minor ticketing errors, and its parent company signalled it would probably leave the market. No.10 North acted as what Lister calls a local situation room, pulling the combined authority, the Department for Transport and the Treasury together behind eleven and a half million pounds of funding. That let the authority buy National Express's assets, protecting four thousand eight hundred jobs and up to seven hundred and fifty buses a day during a three-year move to franchising.

The bigger agenda is what the government calls the rewiring of the state. A cabinet statement in July committed to moving funding, power and people out of Westminster. Lister notes that less than twenty per cent of UK government spending is controlled below national level, against sixty four per cent in Denmark. October's Budget set out a roadmap for fiscal devolution, including business rates and a share of income tax, and ten more mayoral strategic authorities, taking coverage past ninety per cent of England. Of particular interest for health: the statement commits to aligning the boundaries of health, policing, fire and other services with strategic authorities. A delivery plan is due before the end of this year.

Operationally, there are now more than thirty priority projects, each co-sponsored by a Whitehall director general and a senior official in the relevant mayoral authority, with the prime minister chairing a situation room as the final escalation point. Lister's promise is that a no will be fast and clear, rather than an interminable maybe.

He is candid that culture change is the hardest part, describing Downing Street as a shrine to centuries of certain ways of working. No.10 North moves to its own offices on First Street in Manchester next spring, is recruiting fifty more staff on top of a sixty-strong surge team, and Lister says the cost is net neutral, with budgets absorbed from other departments.

Three LLM inflection points in twelve months (Simon Willison)

Willison, the developer and prolific AI blogger, turns his recent keynote at the AI Engineer Code Summit into an essay arguing that coding agents have passed three big thresholds in a single year.

The first was November last year, with Claude Opus 4.5 and OpenAI's GPT-5.1 Codex models. Willison says Opus 4.5 was the first model where, used inside Claude Code, the default result was generally the right thing. Before that he had to correct it so often it did not feel like a clear accelerant. Many people spent the December holidays tinkering, and by January were using these tools for real work.

The second came in June and July with what he calls Fable class models. Anthropic had previewed a model called Mythos in April and said it was too dangerous to release because of its cybersecurity abilities. Claude Fable 5 arrived in June as a defanged version, only to be pulled after three days when US export controls effectively banned it, returning on the first of July. His definition of a Fable class model: if you can define a goal and provide the tools, it can solve your problem by brute force. His example is a mysterious beep in a room full of boxes, which the model located to within about forty centimetres using five microphones. He now counts Fable 5 to 5.2, Opus 5.5, which he calls cheap Fable, and GPT-6 Astra in this class.

The third, in September and October, is 3D modelling and game design. Models suddenly became good at Blender and Three.js. More strikingly, he told Opus 5.5 that a strategy game he was building "isn't fun enough yet. Make it a LOT more fun." After twelve hours of playtesting and rebalancing, it was. He had been telling audiences that making games fun was a uniquely human job, and says that reassurance no longer holds. He notes that the indie games scene has descended into a full-blown culture war over AI.

He ends undecided on whether Fable 5.2 is a fourth inflection point. His test: if you can imagine and clearly describe a piece of software, Fable can build it. His conclusion: "I don't think I'll ever go back to typing most of my code by hand."

Anthropic CEO pushes back on Axios reporting on AI and free will (Axios)

This is a short Axios news piece by Madison Mills, published on Wednesday. Dario Amodei, Anthropic's chief executive, publicly rejected the idea that humans should surrender free will to AI, in response to an Axios story that morning. That story, citing multiple sources, said a commonly held view inside Anthropic is that AI will become so capable that humanity should hand some decision-making to superintelligent systems.

Amodei called the suggestion absurd, saying a constant theme of his writing is that AI must be governed by legitimate democratic processes. His key line: "We should never sacrifice legitimacy or self-determination at the altar of efficiency or security." He drew a distinction between handing governance to AI, which he opposes, and automating particular tasks such as driving a car, designing a cancer drug or writing software, where he said more AI agency will often save lives. He also said it may make sense to slow or halt some deployments, to limit job disruption and AI's ability to build more powerful systems on its own.

Axios stood by its reporting, saying people familiar with company discussions describe the belief as common, and pointing to an old Anthropic product vision slide describing a "utopia ruled by god in a box". Amodei in turn accused rival executives of "vibes-based commercial and political positioning", repeating slogans about human control without changing their products.

The context: Axios describes this as Amodei's most significant public remarks since Anthropic went public the week before, and notes the shares dipped below their listing price on the same day.

The reforms the NHS needs (and those it doesn't) – Daniel Elkeles (FT)

Elkeles, former chief executive of the London Ambulance Service, argues that the NHS does not need more structural reform, but rather different approaches for different groups of patients.

He starts with a story from his time at the ambulance service. A south London GP practice closed, and the neighbouring surgery was swamped with calls. The ambulance service trained its one-one-one team to answer the surgery's phones. A short-term fix became a permanent partnership because, he says, it worked better on four counts: running costs were about the same, calls were answered faster, outcomes improved because call handlers could book GPs, physios or pharmacists, or send an ambulance, and it saved money because people got the right help first time. He recalls a patient who rang their GP rather than nine-nine-nine during a heart attack, and was in surgery within the hour.

He wants this made the national norm, and taken further. A caller's phone number and the NHS app should immediately flag whether they are generally fit and well. If so, they should be offered appointments with GPs anywhere in the country who have capacity at that moment. For older people and those with long-term conditions, the opposite applies: they should be offered one of two named clinicians, making continuity of care the default.

He draws on Surrey Downs Health and Care, where GPs, the hospital, community services, the voluntary sector and the council combined around care for older people, replacing referrals with conversations within a team.

The most pointed section is on end of life. Most people want to die at home or in a hospice, yet hospital is the most common place of death, and hospitals often don't know the patient's wishes. Elkeles wants family doctors to record those preferences and hospitals required to follow them where possible. He insists this is not rationing, but writes of "the absurdity of spending significant sums on people who would rather be made comfortable at home or in a hospice."
