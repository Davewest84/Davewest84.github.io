When AI builds itself — Anthropic

This essay from Anthropic argues that AI is increasingly automating the work of building AI, and that this could tip into what researchers call recursive self-improvement, where AI systems design and train their own successors. The company says this trajectory may arrive sooner than our institutions are ready for, and it carries both enormous promise and serious risk.

The evidence is mostly drawn from inside its own walls. Anthropic engineers shipped roughly eight times more code per quarter in Q2 2026 than they did in the early 2020s. By May 2026, more than 80 per cent of the code merged into production originated from Claude rather than from a human typing it. An internal staff survey in March suggested the median engineer was working about four times faster with the latest models.

The piece also charts how fast raw capability is climbing. The length of task a model can handle on its own has been roughly doubling every four months — a year ago the frontier was a four-minute task; by late last year it was a twelve-hour one. On open-ended coding problems the success rate jumped 50 percentage points in six months. In one April demonstration, AI agents solved an open-ended AI safety problem on their own, recovering almost all of the gap between a weak and a strong supervisor.

Anthropic sketches three possible futures: capability growth plateaus while the technology still disrupts the economy; the labs automate much of the work but humans still set research direction; or full recursive self-improvement takes hold, with progress set purely by available compute and humans no longer steering.

Its stated policy view is striking for a frontier company. It argues that slowing the technology down to buy society more time would probably be beneficial, provided cautious players are not simply outrun by reckless ones. To make any credible global pause possible, it calls for building verification systems so a slowdown could be trusted and enforced — admitting this is harder than any past arms-control regime — and commits to convening policymakers, researchers and civil society on how such coordination might work.

Healthcare's moral emergency: reconnecting healthcare with its mission and purpose — The BMJ

This opinion piece is by Robert Klaber, consultant paediatrician and director of strategy at Imperial College Healthcare Trust, with Maureen Bisognano and Don Berwick, both presidents emerita of the Institute for Healthcare Improvement in Boston. Their argument is that something is wrong in modern healthcare that the metrics do not capture. Health systems have never had greater diagnostic precision, therapeutic power or computing capability, yet patients increasingly feel processed rather than cared for, clinicians speak of moral distress, and the workforce is leaving at an unsustainable rate.

The authors name several structural forces. Berwick's own idea of salve lucrum, the glorification of profit, has become a stranglehold on the moral purpose of care, and they argue no part of the sector is immune. But the distortion runs deeper than money: they describe an industrial model that offers "care for people like you" rather than genuinely caring for you, processing patients through standardised protocols that ignore the texture of individual lives. The BMJ's own Too Much Medicine series gets a nod for documenting the harm of overdiagnosis and overtreatment.

The intellectual spine is a distinction borrowed from Julia Unwin, former chief executive of the Joseph Rowntree Foundation. Public policy, she argues, has two lexicons: the rational — measurement, efficiency, resource — and the relational — feelings, narrative, human connection. The rational has not been wrong, but it has crowded out the relational, and healthcare has lived almost entirely inside the rational for thirty years.

Crucially, the authors insist this is not a soft case but an empirical one. They cite Michael West's landmark study of NHS culture — the largest ever conducted — which found organisations where staff felt supported and valued had consistently lower patient mortality. "Looking after patients requires looking after staff," West concluded. They point to the IHI's framework for joy in work, and to the "What matters to you?" movement, now used in more than 50 countries, as practical, measurable ways to rebalance care. Their closing argument: these currents are powerful but not irreversible, every ward round is a chance to restore the relational alongside the rational, and we need not wait for system reform to begin.
