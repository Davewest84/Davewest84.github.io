Googlebook OS: Google manager Kuscher explains the switch to Android (heise online)

Almost sixteen years after Google launched its first Chromebook, the company is replacing ChromeOS, at least at the premium end of the market, with an Android-based system called Googlebook OS. The German tech site heise online spoke to Alexander Kuscher, Google's senior director of product management for Googlebooks and tablets, about why it has happened now.

The pitch is that Android users, in theory more than three billion people, have never had a desktop operating system that fits their phone. Kuscher says Windows feels out of place, so does the Mac, and even ChromeOS doesn't quite fit. Google is starting with premium users.

Why now, after years of rumours? Kuscher gives two reasons. First, Android simply wasn't mature enough five years ago; making it work on a laptop would have taken a huge engineering effort. It has since grown up, and Google has standardised what it calls adaptive apps, which reflow for different screen sizes. Second, artificial intelligence. Gemini powers features such as the Magic Pointer, a reworked cursor that gives context-aware access to the AI, alongside more mundane conveniences like renaming batches of files.

Kuscher describes it as a multi-purpose operating system, for coding and office work as well as entertainment, with native apps including Photoshop, Lightroom, CapCut and Luminar. He concedes it is early days; hundreds of apps are currently optimised, with more to come.

On hardware, the five Googlebooks so far start at around twelve hundred euros, roughly MacBook Air territory. Cheaper models, under a thousand euros, and pricier ones are promised, though Kuscher would only say it won't take "too long". The consumer electronics show in January is a plausible venue.

He also admits Android's desktop mode, when a phone is plugged into a monitor, still has room for improvement. And on EU regulation, he says Googlebooks would not count as a core platform service under the Digital Markets Act, so Google would not be obliged to open the system to rival AI providers. Even so, he says it should be as open as macOS or Windows, and notes that it already supports Claude running in the terminal.
