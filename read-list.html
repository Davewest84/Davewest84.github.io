ChatGPT Health — Robert Wachter, Pattern Recognition

Wachter opens with the graveyard of big tech in healthcare. He sat on Google Health's advisory board from 2007, until Eric Schmidt walked into a meeting and told the group the whole thing was being disbanded, saying simply, “this is too hard for us”. Amazon's Haven venture with JPMorgan and Berkshire Hathaway, run by Atul Gawande, was killed a few years after launch. IBM sold Watson Health for parts to private equity in 2022 at a multi-billion-dollar loss. His argument is that generative AI has finally changed the odds, and that OpenAI's new patient-facing tool, ChatGPT Health, is close to getting it right.

Wachter's two long-standing objections to patients using general chatbots for health questions were that importing your own record was clunky, so answers came back context-free, and that the tools were not doctor-like enough in how they worked up a symptom. He cites an Oxford study finding GPT-4o produced incorrect diagnoses or triage advice in roughly two-thirds of cases, largely because patients, with no clinician guiding them, left out or distorted key features. He says ChatGPT Health addresses both. He imported his own record from the UCSF Epic portal easily, and tested it on a genuinely tricky case of his own: a brief neurological event raising the question of whether to take daily aspirin, complicated by a previous subdural bleed and a chronically low platelet count. The tool found the relevant labs and notes and reached a conclusion not materially different from two expert physicians. He notes OpenAI does not operate under HIPAA, so importing your data carries real risk, and he thinks the tools will soon replicate a significant chunk of primary and urgent care.

The second half is the Winters case. A 55-year-old Florida former pastor spent more than a year in rambling conversations with GPT-4o about non-specific symptoms. The bot told him “God did not design your body to endlessly fail”, and advised him to spend much of his time “reclining” at home, at points discouraging him from seeking care and from listening to worried friends and family. He was later hospitalised with a massive pulmonary embolism. The suit, filed in San Francisco, personally names Sam Altman and seeks an injunction pausing direct-to-consumer health products until independent safety audits are done. Wachter's reading is that this is less a diagnostic error case than a sycophancy case, closer to the Bing “Sydney” episode than to the hundreds of medical mistakes he has reviewed.

His framing for regulators is the Waymo comparison. Waymo built a reservoir of trust and safety evidence before it went driverless, so when one of its cars killed a well-loved Mission District cat, the backlash was survivable. Cruise, without that reservoir, was out of business within a year of dragging an injured pedestrian. On the standard patient-facing AI should be held to, he quotes one of Joe Biden's favourite lines: “Don't compare me to the Almighty, compare me to the alternative.” He does not think FDA certification of every tool is feasible, and argues instead, with Zeke Emanuel and Alon Bergman in JAMA, for something closer to physician licensure and board certification, assessing training, performance on standardised tests, privacy, and bias including undisclosed conflicts of interest.

How AI-native companies turn workflows into operating capability — OpenAI

This is a corporate piece, published on 1 September 2026, but the underlying data point is worth having. OpenAI's Enterprise Signals work finds that frontier firms, meaning the top 10 per cent by AI usage, now generate 8.3 times as many output tokens per active user as typical firms, up from 2.6 times in January. The gap is widening fast, and OpenAI's reading is that it reflects an operating shift rather than just heavier usage. Leading firms connect agents to company context and tools, delegate more substantive work, and make successful workflows easy to repeat.

It illustrates this with three startups. Basis, which builds agents for accounting firms, cut first-day onboarding from two hours to 30 minutes by demonstrating the process once and turning it into a reusable skill with a clear trigger, known steps, tool access and a definition of “done”. When exceptions crop up, HR updates the skill before the next cohort. Clay, a go-to-market software company, gave every sales account its own persistent workspace and dedicated subagent, which reviews primary sources and updates its deal folder overnight. A coordinating agent then produces a short list of priority moves each morning. It saves about an hour of inbox triage a night, and crucially the supporting evidence stays attached to each recommendation so the seller can inspect the sources before acting. Exa Labs, which builds search infrastructure, turned its developer integration work into a defined workflow where Codex monitors for opportunities, gathers context, opens pull requests, runs tests and drafts announcements, with human review before anything ships.

The closing section is six steps, and the useful ones are about measurement and boundaries. Pick one consequential end-to-end workflow that repeats often enough to learn from. Name an accountable owner, a KPI, a baseline and guardrails, and track both depth, meaning completed tasks and review load, and value, meaning cycle time, quality, cost or risk. There is a nice line on this: “Output volume can show that people are asking AI to do more; workflow outcomes show whether it matters.” Then write the agent's job description, specifying what triggers the work, what evidence it must produce, and where it must stop for human review. One incidental finding worth noting for anyone running a team: six months after adoption, early-career employees were sending 13 more messages a week than executives.
