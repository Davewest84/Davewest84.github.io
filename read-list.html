Devolution and social care are inextricably linked (LabourList, Rosie Fogden, ILC UK)

Fogden's argument is that the government's two big domestic ideas, devolution to drive growth in every postcode and a National Care Service for older people, have been run as separate agendas with separate audiences, when in fact the social care crisis is quietly undermining the growth ambition.

The evidence comes from new ILC research, Inequality Street. Rather than a postcode lottery, she says, it reveals a postcode penalty. Older people in the worst-off areas have a quarter fewer good-quality care options, relative to need, than those in the best-off. Unpaid carers fill the gap: nearly twice the proportion of residents in the worst-off areas provide 20 or more hours of care a week. Where unpaid caring is highest, economic inactivity among 50 to 64-year-olds is highest too.

People in places such as Tameside, Knowsley and Halton also pay more for less. The adult social care precept costs a Band D home 12% more in the worst-off areas, yet raises 40% less per older person in poor health. Comparing Tameside with Wokingham, older people in Tameside are sicker for longer but have around a third of the good-quality care options relative to need.

Her line is blunt: "We can't wait for a National Care Service in the next Parliament." She proposes stop-gap measures. First, the centre should pick up a bigger share of care costs in the poorest places, with funding allocated on population health rather than simply the number of older people, because poor health drives most of the penalty. Second, mayors and strategic authorities should target new employment support powers at over-50s in areas with many family carers. Third, social care should be folded into emerging neighbourhood health services, meaning NHS commissioners, councils and jobcentres working far more closely. Devolved authorities, she argues, are well placed to force that conversation, since they already have a legal duty to improve population health, and good care is itself prevention.

A short piece, essentially a trailer for the ILC report, but the funding-follows-health-need point and the link between care supply and older workers' economic inactivity are the bits worth noting.
