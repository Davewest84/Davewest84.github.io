Google VP John Maletis confirms Googlebooks will run Android apps far better than Chromebooks

Chrome Unboxed reports on comments from Google vice president John Maletis that go to the heart of why Chromebooks have always felt like second-class citizens for Android apps. The problem, historically, has been emulation. On a Chromebook an Android app does not run natively — it runs inside a virtualised container on top of Chrome OS, and that translation layer brings overhead: stuttering, sluggishness, heavier battery drain, and an experience that never quite matches a phone or tablet.

Google's answer with Googlebooks is to drop the emulation layer entirely. These devices are built directly on Android's native architecture, so apps talk to the hardware directly. Maletis said they now have "an ability to run truly native Android applications, not emulated. So performance of these apps is incredible." This holds regardless of the chip inside — Intel, Qualcomm or MediaTek.

The other half of the story is developer enthusiasm. Maletis said developers can see a vision where they are "getting much, much closer to the point where an app developer can build once and deploy across form factors." That build-once, run-everywhere promise has long been the holy grail. By removing a legacy bottleneck, Google is positioning Googlebooks as a genuine laptop alternative rather than a compromise — and says developer momentum is unlike anything Chrome OS devices have seen before.

USA Today brings AI into real newsroom workflows

This is a Microsoft customer story, so read it with that in mind, but the underlying use case is genuinely interesting for anyone running a newsroom. USA Today and parent company Gannett have put AI agents to work on one of the most tedious but important jobs in investigative journalism: public records requests. The agent, built on Microsoft 365 Copilot and woven through Teams, Outlook, SharePoint and OneDrive, helps reporters draft, shape and route freedom-of-information and state records requests.

The workflow keeps a human firmly in charge. A journalist brings a story question, the agent helps turn it into a properly structured request and points it at the right agency, and the journalist reviews and sends it. Thomas Elia, a newsroom leader at the Palm Beach Post, stressed the value of taking time to compose a request that is "carefully written, and routed to the right agency." Stephen Harding, a senior product manager at the USA Today Network, was blunt about the guardrails: "AI is a tool. It's not in charge… We're still in control."

The piece also reaches across the Atlantic to Newsquest, where Calum Banister and Jody Doherty-Cove describe similar work — and the outcome there is concrete: Newsquest credits agent-enabled records requests with five or six front-page stories. The thread running through it all is that editorial judgement and accountability stay with the journalists, while the agent removes friction from the plumbing.

Doing More with Home Automations — Google Home & Nest Community

An announcement from the Google Home and Nest community team, posted by community manager Nandini T, expanding the starters, conditions and actions available when you build automations in the Home app. On security and access control you can now arm and disarm systems, check whether a system is armed, and monitor door locks for specific states (locked, unlocked, jammed, forced open or ajar), plus binary sensors for contact, leaks or freezing.

For appliances you get start, stop, pause and resume control over washers, dryers and coffee machines, plus commands to dock, pause or resume a robot vacuum. Lighting gains brightness, colour and colour-temperature control; window coverings can be opened, closed or checked by position; and thermostats can now report humidity as well as temperature. There is also media playback and volume monitoring, battery and charging status, and smart-switch inputs that distinguish an initial press, a long press and a release.

To make it tangible, the team shared their own everyday automations: an IKEA switch by the bed that kills all the bedroom lamps in one button without waking a sleeping partner; fans on smart outlets that kick in when heat or humidity climbs; a kitchen speaker that announces the weather at 8am; and living-room lights that glow red at 8.30pm on school nights as a no-words-needed cue for the kids to head to bed. Google says these are only in the latest automations editor for now but are coming to Ask Home and Help me create soon, with more capabilities promised month over month.
