Neighbourhood health in England can learn from successful international models (The BMJ)

This is a short, punchy comment from Beccy Baird, a senior fellow at the King's Fund. She looks at Brazil's Family Health Strategy and Healthy Families New Zealand, and her central point is that their success came not from the model itself but from the conditions that let it take root and last.

She picks out four of those conditions. The first is clarity of purpose. Brazil set out to make proactive, neighbourhood based care the default for its whole population. New Zealand's aim was community led change to reduce health inequalities. Different goals, but both were explicit. Baird worries that in England, neighbourhood health risks becoming, in her words, "a broad label applied to a range of short term and long term agendas." Even if the immediate priority is taking pressure off hospitals, she says services should be designed from the start to support longer term aims, like tackling inequalities and shifting control to communities.

The second condition is sustained money. Brazil's national funding let the programme scale and become part of the national health system. New Zealand's multiyear investment gave local systems room to evolve. She contrasts this sharply with the NHS's short term funding cycles and shifting priorities, and writes that "work that depends on building relationships, trust, and community capability cannot be delivered to annual deadlines." Her conclusion is that the shift will need a previously unseen political and financial commitment over many years.

The third is relational roles. In Brazil, community health workers make regular visits to every household in their patch, not just to people who are unwell. New Zealand uses what it calls system connectors to bring organisations and community leaders together. England has similar ambitions behind roles like social prescribing link workers, but Baird says these are often short term funded and poorly integrated. Integrated neighbourhood teams could help fix that.

The fourth is alignment with the wider system: funding models, workforce and, critically, measurement. Her warning here is that if neighbourhood health is judged only on short term activity outputs, it might never be seen as successful. She closes by saying that without that alignment, neighbourhood health risks being "a small scale reorganisation of teams" rather than a real transformation.

Call for bold new ideas to support disabled people and those with health conditions into work (GOV.UK)

Published on the first of October, this launches bidding for the Pathways to Work Innovation Fund, worth sixty million pounds overall. Businesses, charities, public bodies and innovators across the UK can bid to test new ways of helping disabled people and people with health conditions to get into work, stay in it and progress. The fund was first announced in July, opened for bids on the twenty eighth of September, and applications close on the twenty sixth of October.

The examples the department gives are quite tech heavy: AI powered job matching tools, prosthetics, and digital therapies for mental illness. Companies showcased at the launch include Big Health, which offers digital therapy for insomnia, anxiety and depression, and Beam, an AI caseworker platform the release says has helped over seven thousand people with health conditions into work.

An expert panel including the Paralympian Baroness Tanni Grey-Thompson, Mencap chief executive Jon Sparkes and Andrew Greenway of Public Digital will help design the fund and shortlist bids. The fund is also backed by Sir Charlie Mayfield, the former John Lewis boss who wrote the Keep Britain Working review. He is quoted saying economic inactivity caused by health conditions costs the economy two hundred and twelve billion pounds a year, "but this is fixable."

Work and pensions secretary Pat McFadden says the government wants the best ideas from business, charities and disabled people themselves. The release places the fund inside the wider welfare reform package: rebalancing Universal Credit, a Right to Try Work guarantee, three and a half billion pounds for employment support, more face to face health benefit assessments, and a fraud and error crackdown. It also mentions that almost eight hundred organisations attended a first engagement event. One observation of my own, not from the release: several of the showcased firms sell digital mental health and NHS compliant software, so it's possible this money ends up overlapping with NHS talking therapies territory.

Introducing dots (OpenAI)

Dots are OpenAI's new always on AI agents, launched on the twenty ninth of September. They run on a model called GPT six Astra, each has its own cloud computer and browser, and through OpenAI's plugin ecosystem they can connect to more than four thousand apps. The pitch is that a dot gets to know your preferences and standards, works towards your goals around the clock, and sometimes does work before you think to ask. You can message or call your dot in ChatGPT, Slack or Teams, and it carries context across all of them.

The post gives worked examples: a dot that watches customer feedback for an app, then builds and tests fixes and hands the developer finished pull requests; one that reruns a scientist's analyses as new data arrives and updates the figures; one that takes an interview transcript and drafts clips, show notes and social posts for a content creator. That last one is the nearest to journalism. There's also a charming anecdote about a tester's dot noticing he had forgotten to invoice a publication, preparing the invoice and sending it once he approved.

A big chunk of the post is about control. While you're not actively using it, the dot does what OpenAI calls "proactive research" in your connected apps, but with read only tools, so it can't send messages or change anything. Custom rules let you allow, require approval for, or block particular actions, and an auto review system checks risky actions against your instructions. Some things, like changing a password, always stay with the human. OpenAI says it doesn't train directly on a dot's background research or its notes to itself.

There's also a preview of specialist dots for organisations, with their own identity and credentials, tested inside OpenAI on procurement, invoice processing and customer support, and being integrated with Microsoft's Agent 365. Dots are rolling out to Pro and Business Premium users now, with a beta for Enterprise, Edu and Healthcare workspaces. Your first dot comes at no extra cost on those plans, and conversations with it don't count towards ChatGPT usage limits.

Build plugins for Claude with the directory submission portal (Anthropic)

This is a short developer announcement, so a short summary. Anthropic now describes plugins as the main way for third parties to extend Claude. A plugin packages MCP connectors, agent skills, or both, and in Claude Code can also include commands, hooks and agents. There's a new submission portal, open to developers on paid Claude plans. You can submit either a single remote MCP server or a full plugin bundle hosted on GitHub. Each submission is automatically validated and safety scanned, you can track its review status and feedback, and once approved you choose when to publish it to the Claude directory.

After launch, developers get usage analytics, including installs by product and version, how often their listing is viewed, and which searches lead people to it. The post also says Claude supports the latest MCP specification, known as MCP two point oh, with extensions for interactive interfaces inside chat and for zero touch sign in for enterprise users.

Over the coming weeks a single discovery experience will roll out across Claude and Claude Code. Existing skills, connectors and plugins in the directory don't need any changes. Read alongside the OpenAI piece, it's a reminder that both companies are now competing hard on ecosystems of add ons, OpenAI claiming four thousand connected apps for dots, and Anthropic building out a curated marketplace.
