Oh Lord, AI Reporters Are Actually Breaking Big News (Wired)

Kate Knibbs, writing on 12 August, opens with a confession dressed up as a news line. At the Black Hat security conference in Las Vegas, OpenAI gave a surprise talk disclosing new detail about a recent hacking incident, including the odd fact that its rogue AI agents had chatted about their attack on a message board. Reporters in the room scrambled. Wired was one of the fastest to publish. And it still got beaten, by more than three hours, by an outlet called RuntimeWire, which had nobody at the convention centre at all, because it has no writers at all.

RuntimeWire is run by a serial entrepreneur in Austin called Ryan Merket, who puts his own byline on stories his synthetic team produces. He spotted an OpenAI executive posting about the conference on X, fed the live stream transcript to his agents while the session was still running, and published roughly six minutes later. Most of the time he is even more hands off. His tools find the stories as well as drafting, editing, fact checking, illustrating and promoting them. If an agent scores a story as low legal risk, an AI editor publishes it without him reading it first. Since May the site has put out nearly 2,000 stories, crawling court databases, forums, filings and social feeds. It costs about $100 a day. Merket told Wired, and this is the detail that lingers, that he ran the whole operation from his phone while camping in Big Bend National Park: “I put out over 80 articles that week.”

Knibbs is fair about the quality. Flatly written, info-dump texture, a typo in a subhead, and the OpenAI piece oddly emphasised the wrong fact. But the hits pull audiences comparable to midsize tech sites. She finds a second operator too: a BlackRock analyst running an “agentic newsroom” called The Dissent, with invented reporter personalities and a budget under $1,000 a month.

The sceptics get their say. Northwestern’s Nicholas Diakopoulos calls it an experimental phase and doubts there is much audience yet, though his forthcoming research found that ChatGPT and Claude surfaced AI-written sources 16 per cent of the time across four test topics, which may be how this material finds human readers after all. Pete Pachal draws the line where you would expect. Cultivating the trust of a source, he says, is going to be human only. But blogging a live product launch or mining a large dataset for scoops, he thinks, is a natural evolution: “Honestly, it feels a bit inevitable.”

The most revealing moment is at the end. Merket says he follows journalistic ethics, seeks comment, links sources and has issued three corrections. Then he mentions he retracted two accurate scoops about startups because the founders asked him to. Not because they were wrong. As a favour. Founder to founder.

TCL’s Nxtpaper 11 Plus has stolen my heart at MWC 2025 (Trusted Reviews)

This is Lewis Painter’s column from Mobile World Congress in Barcelona in March 2025, and it is a straightforward enthusiasm piece about a screen. TCL is best known for televisions but has been quietly persisting with a proprietary display technology called Nxtpaper, and Painter’s argument is that the fourth generation of it, in the Nxtpaper 11 Plus tablet, is the point at which it stops being a curiosity and starts being a genuine threat to Apple, Samsung and Honor in the mainstream tablet market.

The trick is that the 11.5-inch 2.2K panel can switch between three states: a normal colour screen, a colour e-ink style screen, and black-and-white e-ink. There is a physical button to cycle between them, with what he describes as fun on-screen transitions. The fourth-generation improvements are a laser-based process for the matte finish and noticeably better clarity, especially around the edges of text, which he identifies as the big weakness of the previous version.

Inevitably there is AI in it. An eye-care mode is supposed to switch modes automatically according to what is on screen, so an ebook renders in black and white, gallery photographs in colour e-ink, and YouTube in the ordinary display mode. Painter is more taken with the calibration, which avoids preset profiles in favour of showing you sets of images and asking which look right to your eyes, leaving you with something tuned to your own vision.

His conclusion is that with the matte finish and the optional stylus, the thing becomes something like a Remarkable Pro or a Kindle Scribe, but with the processing power and the app library of a normal Android tablet. The internals are modest: a MediaTek Helio G100 with 8GB or 12GB of memory, 256GB of storage, an 8,000mAh battery and 33W charging. He would have liked a higher specification, but the price is £249, which is rather the point.

Acer Googlebook is officially coming soon, and here’s a first glimpse (Android Authority)

This is a short news item by Hadlee Simons, published on 2 September, and it carries an update that undercuts its own premise, so it is worth taking the two halves separately.

The substance is that Acer used its IFA event in Berlin to confirm that its first Googlebook is “coming soon”, without giving any availability detail, and showed a brief glimpse of the machine. What can be seen from the images is limited but suggestive. It looks like a conventional clamshell laptop rather than a detachable tablet, and there is a Google Pixel phone sitting next to it in the shot, which Simons reads as a nod to the cross-device integration that has already been trailed. Earlier leaks point to an Intel Core Ultra 5 processor, 16GB of memory, a 256GB SSD and a 14-inch OLED screen running at 120Hz, though he is careful to say higher-specified variants cannot be ruled out.

The original piece speculated that we would learn more at Googlebook briefings expected later in the month. The update, added the same afternoon, says Google contacted Android Authority to clarify that there will be no public announcements of any Googlebook hardware on 15 September. So the timing guess was wrong, and the honest summary is that Acer has confirmed it is making one, has shown a photograph, and has said nothing about when.

Simons notes this was not Acer’s only IFA announcement. The company also showed an ePaper monitor, a concept handheld called Project DualPlay Mini, new Swift laptops and new Iconia Android tablets.
