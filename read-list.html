What are think tanks for anyway? (Chaitanya Kumar)

Kumar's starting point is that think tanks, for all their closeness to power, are about to be badly disrupted by AI, because most of what they produce is secondary research layered with political ideology, and the research part can now be done in a fraction of the time and cost. To find out how much, he used Claude to try to replicate four recent papers, from Onward, the Resolution Foundation, the Institute for Fiscal Studies and IPPR. His summary: the data crunching in every paper replicated quickly and cheaply, sometimes in under an hour. The numbers that rest on judgement, a modelling choice, an ambiguous definition or an unstated assumption, did not.

Onward's energy paper is his home turf. He rebuilt the diagnostic half in about an hour, and all thirteen figures he checked held up. The headline half came from a commercial power-system model he couldn't rerun. The Resolution Foundation's work on targeting energy bill support, rebuilt from raw survey microdata using a free open-source tax-benefit model, came out close to the original in about twenty minutes. But it needed six or seven data patches that he says he would never have known to ask for.

The IFS paper on the two-child limit is the instructive failure. In a field he barely knew, the counts and costs reproduced well, but his estimate of children lifted out of poverty came out between a fifth and a third lower. After several hours of Claude generating hypotheses that each collapsed, he gave up. His lesson is that scrutiny still needs someone who knows the field.

The IPPR paper, the source of the much-quoted eight million jobs at risk from AI, used GPT-4 to classify tasks. Kumar's own blind coding agreed with Claude far less well than IPPR's human check did with GPT-4. Reading the prompt's subjective criteria strictly rather than leniently moved the exposure estimate by twenty seven percentage points. And IPPR's thirteen per cent GDP gain was roughly double his like-for-like estimate of six point nine.

His conclusions: leaner think tanks, value shifting to advocacy, fieldwork and judgement, and more mutual scrutiny, ideally with funders requiring replication packages. When a language model is used as a measuring instrument, he argues, publish the prompts and model version and validate against human coding. He also warns that cheap analysis is cheap for bad-faith actors too. More output does not mean more impact, he notes, and what is left is "ideas with a political direction, networks, and access to people in or near power".
