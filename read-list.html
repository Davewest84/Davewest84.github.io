Devolution and social care are inextricably linked (LabourList, Rosie Fogden and Ben Franklin)

Their argument is that the Burnham government has two big agendas that it treats as separate. One is devolution to drive what it calls good growth in every postcode. The other is a National Care Service. Fogden and Franklin say the two are tightly bound together, and that without action the social care crisis will undermine the growth ambitions.

The evidence comes from new ILC research called Inequality Street. Rather than a postcode lottery, they describe a postcode penalty. Older people in the worst-off areas have a quarter fewer good-quality care options, relative to need, than those in the best-off areas. Unpaid carers fill the gap. Nearly double the proportion of residents in the worst-off areas provide twenty or more hours of care a week, and where unpaid caring is highest, economic inactivity among fifty to sixty four year olds is highest too.

The funding mechanism makes it worse. The adult social care precept costs a Band D home twelve per cent more in the worst-off areas, yet raises forty per cent less for every older person in poor health. They contrast Tameside, in the worst-off fifth of their index, with Wokingham in the best-off. Older people in Tameside are sicker for longer, yet have around a third of the good-quality care options relative to need.

Their point is that the country can't wait for a National Care Service in the next Parliament, so they propose stop-gaps. First, central government should carry a bigger share of care costs in the poorest places, with funding that follows need. Crucially, they say most of the penalty is driven by poor health rather than the sheer number of older people, so health outcomes should drive allocation. Second, mayors should play a part. Most have no social care responsibility, but strategic authorities are getting more employment support powers, and the authors want those targeted at over-fifties in areas with lots of family carers. They also argue devolved authorities are well placed to force NHS commissioners, councils and jobcentres together around neighbourhood health, noting they already have a legal duty to improve population health, and that, in their words, "good care is itself prevention."

Scotland was years ahead of England in prevention spending targets, but there's time to catch up (Siva Anandaciva)

He starts with a document he says he keeps rereading: the Scottish Government's Health and Social Care medium term financial framework from twenty eighteen. He calls it "one of the most quietly radical things I've read", because it set a big hairy audacious goal to shrink the share of frontline NHS spending going on hospitals to under half within five years. England's Ten Year Health Plan followed suit some seven years later, promising "a decisive shift in the pattern of health spending" away from hospitals, delivered over the next three to four years.

He draws four lessons. First, the NHS makes clear commitments, but only some get high-profile tracking. When the waiting time targets came in you couldn't move for performance data, yet he asks where the metrics are for this spending shift, and suggests it isn't the kind of thing being pored over in delivery unit stocktakes. Second, these pledges are easier to make than deliver, and nobody says what will be given up. Moving care out of hospital is worth doing, but saving money isn't one of the reasons, and hospital care will suffer as a consequence. Third, timing. He used to think the right moment was when hospital targets were being met and cash was plentiful. Given the four hour and eighteen week targets haven't been met for over a decade, he now wonders whether it's time to stop colluding with the idea they can be recovered and accept new measures of what good looks like. Fourth, overlapping initiatives confuse things. The new Preventative Departmental Expenditure Limits from the Autumn Budget are about prevention, which is not the same as moving care. Home dialysis changes where care happens; it doesn't prevent the need for it.

He sets out the three shifts and what's unclear about each: a ten billion pound digital fund with hazy detail, a hospital-to-community shift with no target or deadline for the hospital share, and a prevention spending category with less than a year to define what counts. Still, he ends optimistic, quoting James Frick: "Don't tell me what your priorities are. Show me where you spend your money, and I'll tell you what your priorities are."

Learning from experience of devolution in health and social care (Chris Ham)

Ham surveys nearly thirty years of devolution. Northern Ireland's power sharing brought relative peace but made major reform hard to agree, so successive health reviews went unenacted and performance lagged. Scotland used its powers for free personal care, free prescriptions and minimum unit pricing, but withdrew its national care service plans after opposition from councils and unions. Wales prioritised prevention and its future generations legislation, initially raising social care spending faster than the NHS before reversing that under health pressures. All three built integration bodies similar to England's integrated care systems, with limited evidence of benefit.

On outcomes, he cites the Institute for Fiscal Studies finding that English hospitals outperformed Scotland and Wales post-pandemic on activity and waiting lists, while Scotland did best on A and E waits. He makes a striking observation: one paradox of devolution is that power within the devolved nations ends up centralised in their national governments, partly because of heavier scrutiny by their own parliaments.

On Greater Manchester, he describes the health side of Devo Manc as soft or constrained devolution, because NHS target setting and performance management stayed in place. Since becoming an integrated care board it has faced more hierarchical oversight, which one study said risked crowding out partnership working.

His three lessons. Macro pressures like money and Covid limit how far nations can diverge. The language misleads: in Manchester, he writes, "administrative delegation is a more accurate description of what happened than devolution", and "the fine print is a more reliable guide to what happens than press release headlines." And leadership capability has been badly neglected, which he links to Welsh Labour's defeat at the twenty twenty six Senedd elections. He urges the Prime Minister to match his personal commitment to devolution with investment in the people who have to deliver it.
