Read-list roundup

Updated Thursday 20 August 2026 · 2 pieces

A new book looks at how AI is rewiring the newsroom, for better and worse – Nieman Journalism Lab

An extract from Journalism in the Age of AI by Seth Lewis, Rodrigo Zamith and Tomás Dodds (Polity). The authors' central argument is blunt: AI is reshaping journalism, but the way the two are developing together right now is not working. In their words, it is “largely accelerating a broken system that has been grinding journalists and public trust down for decades”. The extract walks through five stages of news production where AI is already changing practice.

On story ideation, they describe newsrooms using AI to spot stories that fit familiar templates. Sisi Wei at CalMatters says her organisation's system “tracks pretty much everything we can track about California's state legislators, parses that fire hose of data, and then generates story ideas for our reporters”, some of which “would have taken a data-savvy political reporter weeks, if not months, to find on their own”. The authors are more interested in AI as a “serendipity engine”: something that deliberately introduces productive friction into a journalist's routine and surfaces non-obvious connections, rather than replicating existing editorial judgement.

On sourcing, they are careful about the limits. AI can audit who gets quoted and flag imbalance, and research shows automated systems can do this accurately. But journalists' reliance on government sources does not stem from limited imagination, it stems from the authority those voices confer. Current tools are best at finding sources who are already digitally present, which risks reinforcing the hierarchy rather than challenging it.

On verification, they quote researcher Henk van Ess: “Traditional fact-checking takes hours or days. AI misinformation generation takes minutes.” Full Fact's Kate Wilkinson describes the hybrid model, where AI finds and filters claims at scale while human checkers provide “the expertise, judgment and accountability”. A study of 22 fact-checkers found existing tools often failed to fit workflows and produced results that increased rather than reduced workload.

The storytelling section covers the familiar disasters: Sports Illustrated's fake AI bylines, CNET's 77 machine-generated stories, and a Florida nonprofit where the editor-in-chief's undisclosed AI editing tools inserted hallucinated quotes into reporters' drafts. But it also covers vibe coding as genuine upskilling. Joe Amditis at the Center for Cooperative Media, who does not call himself a software developer, has built dashboards, interactive features and web tools for New Jersey local newsrooms: “the most valuable skills for vibe coding are the skills of a good project manager… You're a manager now, delegating the coding to an LLM.”

The distribution section ends on the structural point. However sophisticated newsroom AI becomes, it cannot liberate publishers from platform dependency. Newsrooms still cannot alter the decisions platform owners make about moderation, algorithmic demotion of news, or sudden rule changes.

ChatGPT Health – Robert Wachter, Pattern Recognition

Wachter opens with the graveyard. Google Health, whose advisory board he sat on, was disbanded with Eric Schmidt walking into the room and saying “this is too hard for us”. Amazon's Haven venture with JPMorgan and Berkshire Hathaway, run by Atul Gawande, was pulled after a few years. IBM sold Watson Health for parts in 2022 at a multi-billion-dollar loss. His argument is that generative AI has finally changed the odds.

The piece is a hands-on review of OpenAI's new patient-facing tool, ChatGPT Health (distinct from its clinician product). Wachter says his two long-standing objections to recommending general chatbots to patients have now been addressed. The first was context: importing your own records was clunky, and without them you get a context-free answer. “A sore throat in a healthy patient is very different from one in an immunocompromised patient.” The second was that the tools were not doctor-like enough in how they interrogate a symptom. He cites an Oxford study finding GPT-4o produced incorrect diagnoses or triage advice in roughly two-thirds of cases, often because patients, with no clinician guiding them, omitted or distorted key features.

He imported his own record from his UCSF Epic/MyChart portal easily, noting that OpenAI does not operate under HIPAA, so handing over your data carries real risk. He tested it on a genuinely tricky question of his own: whether to take daily aspirin after a hard-to-diagnose neurological event, against a record including a small subdural bleed and a chronically low platelet count. The tool found the relevant labs and notes and reached a conclusion “not materially different” from the two expert physicians he had already consulted. His verdict: these tools will soon replicate a significant chunk of primary and urgent care, more conveniently and more cheaply.

The second half is about liability. In the same week, OpenAI was hit with what may be the first lawsuit alleging AI-induced misdiagnosis, brought over a 55-year-old Florida former pastor who developed a “massive” pulmonary embolism after months of rambling exchanges with GPT-4o. Wachter is honest that it is a poor test case: the symptoms were non-specific, and the claim that immobility advice caused the clot is “clinically dubious”. What it really illustrates, he thinks, is sycophancy, a model telling an emotionally invested user what he wanted to hear, including the line “God did not design your body to endlessly fail”.

His framing of the regulatory question is the part worth stealing. He borrows a Biden line: “Don't compare me to the Almighty, compare me to the alternative.” The benchmark should not be a perfect physician but the highly imperfect status quo, where for many people the alternative is no physician at all. He does not think FDA certification of every patient-facing tool is feasible, and argues instead (with Zeke Emanuel and Alon Bergman, in JAMA) for something modelled on physician licensure and board certification: assessing how tools were trained, how they perform on standardised tests, how they protect confidentiality, and how they avoid bias, including bias born of undisclosed conflicts of interest. He also runs a useful analogy from driverless cars: Waymo built a reservoir of safety evidence before its first bad headline and survived; Cruise did not, and was out of business inside a year.
