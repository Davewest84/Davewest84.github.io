Read-list roundup

Updated Saturday 8 August 2026 · 1 piece

Care to Share — building the National Care Service (Fabian Society)

A collection of eleven short essays published in June 2026, edited by Ben Cooper, Iggy Wood and Miles Ward, and supported by the Joseph Rowntree Foundation. The framing throughout is impatience. Labour promised a National Care Service in its manifesto, drawing on the Fabians' own Support Guaranteed report, and the contributors argue that almost two years in, the government has not yet built the foundations.

The most striking contribution is the foreword by Wes Streeting, described here as the former secretary of state for health and social care. He is unusually candid about Labour's caution in opposition: "My plan to boot private equity sharks out of social care was cut from the manifesto, as was a proposal for a royal commission." He says Downing Street delay means the Casey commission is not due to report until 2028, which "risks scuppering the chance of proper reform before the general election", and predicts that social care "will once again be sacrificed on the altar of an election campaign". He also calls for a social care sponsorship body to strip employers of their power over migrant care workers, arguing the current visa system enables "a form of modern slavery in 21st century Britain".

The strongest analytical chapter is by Andrew Harrop, now at Public First and formerly the Fabians' general secretary. He sets out four questions on money. The taxpayer spends around £30bn a year on adult social care in England against £190bn on the NHS, and he argues real spending needs to rise by at least a further £15bn over the next decade. Demand for local authority services is projected to grow 24 per cent in 10 years. The share of over-65s receiving publicly funded care fell from around 8 per cent in the early 2000s to 3.4 per cent by 2021 and has barely recovered. He wants a ring-fenced funding stream for adult care, separate from the rest of local government finance, as with schools. He also floats genuinely difficult revenue options: national insurance on earnings after state pension age, an equivalent levy on private pensions in payment, limits on tax-free pension lump sums, proper taxation of lifetime gifts, and a proportionate property tax.

Several other threads recur. Anna Dixon, Labour MP for Shipley, calls for national commissioning standards to end what she calls an indefensible postcode lottery, plus a 10-year social care workforce plan to sit alongside the NHS one. Louise Woodruff and Abby Jitendra of the Joseph Rowntree Foundation set out how punishing the charging system is: the average cost of a week in residential care is now over £1,000, means-test thresholds have been frozen since 2010, and just under a quarter of a million people a year fund their care entirely themselves. They point to France, Australia, Germany and Japan as places that share those costs more sensibly. Gavin Edwards of Unison reports 110,000 vacancies in the sector, nearly a quarter of the workforce leaving each year, and public satisfaction with social care at just 14 per cent. Samantha Clark of Learning Disability England gives her chapter over to three first-person accounts of care charging, the most affecting section in the pamphlet.

For an HSJ reader the most useful material is the NHS interface. Paulette Hamilton, a former district nurse and now MP for Birmingham Erdington, argues that ambulance queues and delayed discharges are symptoms of social care underfunding rather than health service problems. Sara Hyde warns against over-centralisation and points to integrated care boards as a possible vehicle for regional care commissioning. Ben Cooper's own chapter argues that if the other parties will not agree, Labour should take the argument to the public at the next election rather than wait for consensus.
