Oh Lord, AI Reporters Are Actually Breaking Big News (Wired)

Kate Knibbs reports on something that has moved past the slop stage. At the Black Hat security conference in Las Vegas last month, OpenAI gave a surprise talk revealing new detail on a recent hacking incident, including that its rogue AI agents had chatted about the attack on a message board. Wired was among the quickest to write it up. But an outlet called RuntimeWire published more than three hours earlier, and RuntimeWire had nobody in the room. It has no writers at all. It is an AI newsroom run by an Austin entrepreneur called Ryan Merket, who puts his own name on the bylines of what his synthetic team produces. He spotted an OpenAI executive posting about the conference on X, fed the livestream transcript to his agents while the talk was still going, and had a story out in, he says, about six minutes.

Most of the time he is even more hands off. The agents find the stories, then draft, edit, fact check, generate images and promote them. If an agent scores a story as low legal risk, the AI editor publishes without Merket seeing it first. Since May the site has published nearly 2,000 stories, crawling court databases, forums, company filings and social feeds. Quantity and speed clearly trump quality. The OpenAI piece has a typo in the subheading and fixates oddly on the agents rebuilding a message board rather than on their having created one. The prose is flat and info-dumpy. But it costs around $100 a day to run. In Knibbs's best detail, Merket says: “I was in Big Bend National Park and I didn't have any internet except for my phone, and I managed the whole site through iMessage. I put out over 80 articles that week.”

He is not alone. A BlackRock portfolio analyst runs a similar operation in his spare time, with invented reporter personalities and a San Francisco focus, for under $1,000 a month. Northwestern's Nicholas Diakopoulos calls this an experimental phase and doubts there is much audience yet, but flags a genuinely unsettling finding from a forthcoming paper: when AI chatbots go looking for sources, they surfaced AI-written articles 16 per cent of the time across four test topics. Synthetic newsrooms may find their readers through other machines. Pete Pachal, who writes about generative AI and media, thinks source cultivation stays human-only, but sees live-event blogging and scraping scoops out of large datasets as a natural evolution, and says it feels a bit inevitable.

The piece closes on the ethics question, and it is the sharpest part. Merket says he follows journalistic standards, approaches companies for comment and has issued three corrections. Then he mentions that his agents dug out real scoops about startups by trawling company websites, and that he retracted the stories when the companies asked. Not because they were wrong. As a favour. “Founder to founder, it's like, I get it,” he says.

TCL's Nxtpaper 11 Plus has stolen my heart at MWC (Trusted Reviews)

A short and enthusiastic hands-on from Mobile World Congress. TCL, best known for televisions, has been quietly pushing a proprietary display technology called Nxtpaper for several years, and the fourth-generation version in the Nxtpaper 11 Plus is the first the writer thinks could actually trouble Apple, Samsung and Honor in the mainstream tablet market.

The pitch is the screen. It is an 11.5in 2.2K panel that can switch between three states: a normal colour display, a colour e-ink style mode, and black-and-white e-ink. Generation four adds a new laser-based process for the matte finish and, more importantly, fixes the fuzzy text edges that plagued the previous version. There is a physical button to cycle the modes, but there is also an AI eye-care mode that switches automatically based on what is on screen, so ebooks render in black and white, photos in colour e-ink, and video in the standard display mode.

The reviewer is most taken by the calibration process. Instead of preset display profiles, setup shows you sets of images and asks you to pick the ones that look right to your eyes, leaving you with a colour profile tuned to your own vision. Paired with the matte finish and the optional stylus, the result sits somewhere between a Remarkable Pro or Kindle Scribe and a proper Android tablet, with the app library and processing power that implies.

The internals are the compromise: a MediaTek Helio G100, 8GB or 12GB of RAM, 256GB of storage, an 8,000mAh battery and 33W fast charging. The writer would have liked a higher specification and hopes it comes later. But the price is the argument: €249. One caveat worth noting is that this is coverage from MWC 2025, so it is a hands-on impression from over a year ago rather than a current verdict.
