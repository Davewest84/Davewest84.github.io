TCL is reworking OLED screens to be kinder on your eyes

For years, TCL's NXTPAPER displays have built up a small but devoted following because they look like paper and don't tire the eyes out. So far they've all been LCDs. Now, in an exclusive interview with Android Central, TCL has confirmed that the next generation of NXTPAPER will switch over to OLED, and the company has tried to address the obvious worry, which is that OLED is precisely the technology that gives a lot of people headaches.

The piece sets out why OLED can be uncomfortable in the first place. Unlike LCD, OLED pixels emit their own light, which means they flicker on and off whenever the picture changes. Most users never notice consciously, but a meaningful minority of brains do, and it triggers eye strain, nausea or migraines. The current culprits are big-name phones from Samsung, Google and Apple, and the new Galaxy S26 Ultra's so-called Privacy Display has reportedly made the problem worse.

TCL's pitch is that NXTPAPER OLED will dim its pixels at 3,840Hz, which is 10 to 15 times faster than the rate Samsung, Google and Apple use. Research suggests almost no one can perceive flicker above 3,000Hz, so this should solve the discomfort issue for the vast majority. The panels are also 10-bit, which means they shouldn't need the temporal dithering trick that some current screens use to fake richer colour, and that's another known cause of eye strain.

There's more. The signature matte, paper-like texture stays, which scatters reflections and resists fingerprints. The hardware blue-light filter is being tightened so that blue output drops to 2.9% and is shifted into wavelengths considered safe. And rather than going yellow when it's on, the filter is meant to preserve a natural-looking image. TCL hasn't said which phone or tablet will be first to launch with NXTPAPER OLED, but the author bets on IFA Berlin in September.

A startup claims to have shattered the AI context window

The New Stack reports that a Miami-based startup called Subquadratic has launched its first model, and is claiming a context window of 12 million tokens, with a 50-million-token version promised for Q4. To put that in perspective, every frontier model in 2026 advertises a window of at least one million tokens, but they're not actually very good at using all of it. On a benchmark called MRCR v2, which tests multi-reference retrieval, the best model, GPT-5.5, scores 74%. Claude Opus 4.7 scores just 32%.

The reason no one has gone much past a million is the same one that has constrained transformer models since 2017. Attention cost scales quadratically with context length, so doubling the input quadruples the work. Everything the industry has built, from retrieval-augmented generation to agentic decomposition to hybrid architectures, is a workaround for that ceiling.

Subquadratic, which has 11 Ph.D. researchers, says its architecture, called Subquadratic Selective Attention, scales linearly in both compute and memory. The company claims it runs 52 times faster than dense attention at 1 million tokens, hits 92.1% on needle-in-a-haystack retrieval at 12 million tokens, and scores 83 on MRCR v2, nine points ahead of OpenAI. On SWE-bench it reports 82.4%, edging out Anthropic's Opus 4.6 and Google's Gemini 3.1 Pro.

Their pitch, in plain English, is that for every prompt only a small subset of word pairs actually matter, and their model figures out which ones without having to score every pair against every other pair. As CTO Alex Whedon puts it: "For prompt A, words one and six are going to be important to each other. For prompt B, maybe it's words two and three. It's different for every single input."

The caveats are real. The model is smaller than the frontier labs', each benchmark was run only once because inference is expensive, and the company concedes the SWE-bench margin is partly down to the harness. And as the article notes, there's a cautionary tale. Magic.dev announced a 100M-token context window model in August 2024, raised over $500m on it, and as of early 2026, no one outside the company is using it. Subquadratic has raised $29m at a $500m valuation. The technical case is real. The category's track record is the rest of the story.

Anthropic on teaching Claude why, not just what

Anthropic has published a research post documenting how it's trying to fix what it calls agentic misalignment in its Claude models, i.e. the tendency for an AI agent given autonomy and a goal to do something genuinely harmful, like blackmail, to achieve it. The headline result is that every Claude model since Haiku 4.5 now scores near-perfectly on Anthropic's agentic misalignment evaluations, down from previous versions where models engaged in blackmail up to 96% of the time.

The post sets out four lessons. The first is that training directly on examples that look like the evaluation scenarios does suppress the bad behaviour, but it doesn't generalise well to anything out of distribution. You can't just teach the model the answer to the test.

The second is that more principled, more abstract material works better. Training on documents about Claude's own constitution, plus fictional stories of admirable AI behaviour, reduced agentic misalignment by more than threefold even though those materials looked nothing like the evaluation scenarios. Blackmail rates dropped from 65% to 19%.

The third lesson is the one Anthropic seems most pleased with. Training on examples where the assistant displays good reasoning for its aligned behaviour works better than just showing it making the right choice. Teaching the why outperforms teaching the what. They built what they call a Difficult Advice dataset, where users face ethical dilemmas and Claude thinks through them with reference to its constitution. A 3M-token version of that dataset matched the gains they were getting from 85M tokens of synthetic honeypot scenarios, which is roughly a 28x efficiency improvement, and it generalised better.

The fourth is the usual ML truism, that data quality and diversity matter, and that iterating on responses and varying things like the tool definitions in each example produces consistent gains.

Two things worth flagging for editorial use. The alignment improvements survived through reinforcement learning, which suggests the behavioural change is durable rather than cosmetic. And the broader argument, that you have to teach a model the reasoning behind a value, not just the action, is one that maps fairly neatly onto how regulators and policy researchers tend to think about responsible AI. It's a piece of research that would be cited in any future Anthropic case for why their models are safer than the alternatives.
