Google VP John Maletis on what makes Googlebook different from Android Desktop

Chrome Unboxed sat down with Google vice president John Maletis to settle a question that has been nagging at the tech world for months. Google is simultaneously building two things that look suspiciously similar. One is Android Desktop, a mode that lets you plug a phone or tablet into an external screen and get a windowed, laptop-style interface, and which is getting a big push with Android 17. The other is Googlebook, a new class of computer running its own operating system built on the Android tech stack. If both deliver a desktop experience powered by Android, why have two?

Maletis frames the answer around hardware. When Google designed this category a couple of years ago, it started by asking what genuinely makes a laptop different from a phone or tablet. A laptop is a dedicated, integrated productivity machine with a large screen, a permanent physical keyboard and a trackpad. Rather than just enlarging phone apps to fill a bigger monitor, Google says it used those physical characteristics as the starting point for the operating system, and looked at parts of the laptop that the industry has, in his telling, ignored for decades.

His headline example is a feature called Magic Pointer. The mouse cursor, he argues, has not fundamentally changed in over forty years, and bolting an AI assistant onto the side of an operating system does not change that — you just use the same old pointer to click an AI chat box. With Googlebook, Google is baking Gemini's intelligence directly into the navigation mechanics of the trackpad and cursor, turning the pointer from a simple clicking tool into a contextual assistant that adapts to your workflow in real time. Maletis kept the specifics vague.

The deeper distinction, he says, is architectural. Android Desktop is an optional mode pinned onto a mobile operating system, a temporary workspace you toggle on when you connect a screen. Googlebook is an end-to-end unified platform built from the ground up, with Google working directly with Intel, Qualcomm and MediaTek on optimised silicon and controlling the software stack natively to avoid Android's usual fragmentation. Google is now calling it an intelligence system rather than just an operating system, with the AI woven into the machine to remove friction quietly rather than sitting in a separate app demanding attention. More Googlebook-exclusive features are promised, with the full pitch due to land this autumn.
