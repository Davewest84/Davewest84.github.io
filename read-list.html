Tarp set ups for couples and solos — Wildway Bushcraft

A practical guide to camping under a tarp rather than a tent, aimed at solo campers, couples and small groups. It makes the case for the tarp on two grounds: weight and versatility. The numbers do the arguing. A 3x3m DD tarp is 790g excluding pegs and lines, with a groundsheet adding 174g, so you have a one or two person shelter for well under a kilo. By comparison the Hilleberg Akto, which the piece calls one of the best one-man tents available, weighs 1.6kg. The versatility argument is that a tarp can be folded, as the writer puts it, “origami like, into an almost endless variety of shapes and shelters”, and can double as a windbreak, a hammock cover, or a layer between you and the ground.

Most of the useful content is about site selection. Assuming you have the landowner's permission or are somewhere wild camping is allowed, the location dictates the shelter: Dartmoor rules out hammocks, Scottish forest suits them. Avoid marshy ground, valley bottoms, pitches close to water and places where rivers intersect. Use natural features, so trees at a convenient spacing for a hammock or a lean-to, or boulders and cliffs as part of the structure. On cold spots, the advice is to read windswept treetops for the prevailing wind direction and pitch side-on to it rather than creating a wind tunnel through camp: if the wind runs north to south, your head and feet should point west and east. Pitch midway up an incline, because the top of a hill leaves you exposed and the bottom means you lose the benefit of rising warm air.

On kit, it recommends 3x3m for one person and a 4.5x3m XL tarp for a couple on the ground or a small group. Use proper 550lb breaking strain paracord, because the inner strands can be stripped out for fishing line or sewing thread. The hammock section is blunt about heat loss being the main problem of sleeping off the ground, since compressing your sleeping bag underneath you accelerates it. The fixes offered are a sleeping mat narrower than the hammock, or an underblanket slung below, plus a centre-zip sleeping bag so you can get out quickly, and the usual instruction to layer up.

It closes on multi-person options, and is honest that they are heavy. A Clark Vertex is close to 2kg for the hammock alone and nearer 3kg with tarp and gear; a Tentsile Stingray, which is really a three-person hammock tent, is 9kg, against under 5kg for a typical mid-budget three-person four-season tent. The advanced set-up diagrams are reproduced from Roger Caffin and are free for non-commercial use. Worth noting: the piece is partly a marketing vehicle for Wildway's own weekend bushcraft courses and for DD Hammocks kit, so read the product recommendations in that light.
