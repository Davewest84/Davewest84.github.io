Introducing Claude Duet (Anthropic)

Claude Duet is a new app from Anthropic, published on the twenty first of October, in which Claude works as what the company calls a proactive agent. Rather than waiting to be asked, it comes to you with what needs your attention, completes tasks you hand it, keeps working when you step away, and checks with you before acting on your behalf. It is pitched at professionals and small business owners with more on their plates than they can track. For now it is a research preview, available by waitlist, starting with Pro and Max subscribers in the United States, and it shares the same usage limits as the rest of Claude.

The day to day pattern is simple. Each morning it sends a short brief on the most important items across your connected apps, then checks those accounts every few hours and messages you only when something needs you. Anthropic is candid that it checks every few hours rather than continuously because constant checking would burn through your plan's limits. It runs on its own virtual machine with its own browser, so your laptop or phone does not need to be on, and it carries over your existing memory, skills and connected apps from your Claude account.

The safety section is the most substantive part. Before it sends a message, makes a payment, signs a document or deletes anything, it shows you exactly what it is about to do and waits for approval. Some things are off limits even with approval, such as trading securities or moving money between accounts. A new classifier is meant to block sharing of the most sensitive tier of information, like card numbers and government ID, while health and personal finance details go out only if you have explicitly told it to send them. Purchases go through Stripe's Link wallet using a virtual card.

The testimonials are from small business owners. One estate agent says it caught a property viewing on the wrong day without being asked: "exactly the kind of thing I want caught without having to ask." A restaurant owner with about a hundred and ten staff says, "I don't need to open up a hundred tabs."

Anthropic is upfront about the limits: it might misread a thread or miscalibrate what is urgent, personal errands are weaker than work tasks, and it lives in a separate app from the main Claude app for now.

Cameron Winter: Live at Carnegie Hall (Pitchfork review)

Walden Green reviews the live album from the Geese frontman's solo concert at Carnegie Hall on the eleventh of December last year, released alongside a concert film by Paul Thomas Anderson that premiered at the New York Film Festival. Green's verdict is that the new songs rank among Winter's very best, and that the record is both a faithful rendering of a you had to be there night and a piece of real time mythmaking.

The review opens on Winter as a habitual teller of fibs to journalists, and on the painful, yearlong making of his solo debut, Heavy Metal. Green places the Carnegie Hall show in an exclusive club: very few American musicians were younger than Winter, now twenty four, when they first conquered that stage, among them Laura Nyro, Tim Buckley, Joan Baez and James Taylor.

Stripped back to voice and Steinway, Green argues, Winter gets closer to some platonic ideal of his music. Songs that plodded on the studio album now glide, as if the piano has lungs. Green hears Carole King, Nyro, jazz pianists like Nina Simone and Ahmad Jamal, and half a century of the Great American Songbook compressed into a brilliant point of light. The film, shot largely in close up, shows Winter visibly nervous at the start and hunched so close over the keys that the microphone sometimes loses him, but by a few songs in he has the room, as Green puts it, cradled safely in his palms.

The standout new songs are Emperor the Thirteenth in Shades, with the line "I don't like what happened to Jesus, but I sure do like Saturday morning", which Green says Warren Zevon and Randy Newman would have fought over, and If You Turn Back Now, which turns a breakup song into the raving of a mad preacher.

The closing argument is the most interesting. On stage, the old songs flow out cool and controlled, divorced from the fraught circumstances of their making. They become standards. Green reads that as the essence of showmanship, and the most sustainable way to handle stardom, with Winter's evasiveness in interviews acting as a stopper in the bottle so he does not pour himself out entirely.

What's going on with the early flu season? (Adam Kucharski)

The epidemiologist starts with last autumn's "super flu" headlines in Europe. Flu positivity rose earlier than usual, prompting predictions of the worst season in a decade, but both positivity and hospitalisations ended up peaking lower than in the twenty twenty two to twenty three and twenty twenty four to twenty five winters. Now flu is rising unusually early again, in both the US and the UK, so he tests the four main explanations circulating in news coverage against the data, using Popper's idea that a good theory forbids certain things from happening.

Cooler weather does not fit. If it were the driver, cooler places should rise first. Instead, southern US states like Texas and Georgia rose as early as northern ones, and in England the South rose before the North, with six per cent of tests positive in the South in mid September against four per cent in the North. This September was also warmer than last in the UK.

School return does not fit well either. Visits by school age Americans roughly double every August as other viruses spread too, so that is not unusual. What is unusual is the September rise among over sixty fives. In England, young adults aged fifteen to forty four are leading, from four per cent positivity in mid August to fourteen per cent by late September, while the rise in school age children came later, was smaller, and began during the summer holidays.

Low vaccination does not fit the timing, since flu started rising before most doses are given, and uptake in England is only slightly behind last year.

The best fit is a drifted H1N1 virus. In the US, H1N1 carrying a mutation called G155E went from about ten per cent of sequenced H1N1 samples in June and July to over ninety five per cent in September. Flu is rising in every age group, and a preprint found that antibodies from teenagers and young adults recognise the new strain less well, which is consistent with young adults leading in England.

But there is a catch. In England, the H3N2 subtype has been just as common as H1N1 since mid September, so the new variant is likely only part of the story. His conclusion is that seasonality is rarely as simple as the reports claim.
