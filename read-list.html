Head of Anthropic's Claude Code says prompt engineering not that important

Roger Montti in Search Engine Journal, covering a Y Combinator interview in which Diana Hu questions Boris Cherny, the creator and head of Claude Code at Anthropic.

Cherny's central claim is that prompt engineering — the craft of carefully wording instructions to an AI model — matters far less than people think. A year ago, he notes, prompt engineer was one of the most popular job openings, then the fashion shifted to context engineer, and he expects these waves to keep coming and going. “I think the skill nowadays is less about prompt engineering and more about figuring out how do you give Claude a hard task that seems a little bit too hard? And then how do you make it possible for Claude to verify its work along the way? And the verification, I think, is probably the single most important thing that people do not get right, largely.”

He gives two illustrations. Inside Anthropic, an engineer handed the model access to OpenCV, an open-source computer vision library, and asked it to draw images. It could, which nobody had expected. In the second, Cherny gave the model a Mac virtual machine and asked it to rewrite Anthropic's own desktop app from the Electron framework into Apple's Swift, running the original in the VM, screenshotting it, and comparing pixel by pixel against the new version. His instruction was blunt: “Don't stop until you're done.” He adds that the model then decided to live-blog its own progress, creating an internal Slack channel and posting screenshots every few minutes.

Asked what separates the top 1 per cent of users, Cherny's advice was to stop hunting for tricks. “Maybe like, don't listen to the LinkedIn influencers. Don't listen to, don't read Twitter. This is the thing about the model is I think everyone's looking for like the one weird trick to do it. That doesn't exist, there's nothing like that.” Instead you approach it empirically: give it a task that's too hard, give it the tools to verify the work as you would yourself, watch where it struggles, then fix that — with better prompting, a skill, or a route to pull in missing context.

His sharpest observation is that experience is a liability here. Engineers who have been coding for years or decades share “a really, really common failure mode, is trying to over-specify, and it's trying to be overly specific, and get the model to do the task exactly the way that you would have done it. And that's just not the way the model works.” Unlearning it is a journey, and the right mental model now is to treat it like a coworker.

From lab to newsroom: how Reuters builds AI tools journalists actually use

A WAN-IFRA piece by Neha Gupta: an edited conversation in which Euan Rocha, Bangalore bureau chief at Thomson Reuters, interviews three colleagues — Chris Peters (speed editor), Sri Prasad (senior manager, product management) and Sagar L (manager, applied research, TR Labs).

The concrete detail is the useful part. Reuters has 250–300 journalists across its bureaus and speed teams, publishing around 100,000 business news alerts a month. The flagship tool is Fact Genie, an AI-assisted summarisation tool that scans an entire document in under five seconds and suggests newsworthy alerts, which a journalist then reviews, cross-checks and decides whether to publish. The goal is key alerts within 30 seconds of receiving a press release; Peters says the first alert often goes out within six seconds. Alongside it sit LEON, a headline assistant, and AVISTA, which uses machine learning to help journalists find, tag and edit photos and video. Prasad says Fact Genie went from idea to rollout in about four months, starting deliberately with 10 senior journalists covering the US market, the most critical one, before expanding.

Three findings stand out. On skills: Sagar says they realised “it's easier to teach a senior journalist to write effective prompts than to train a data scientist in journalism”, which led them to pair prompting, journalists and data scientists building solutions together. Second, and genuinely counterintuitive: on a separate tool generating AI synopses for legal editors, they expected time savings, and junior editors did work faster, but senior editors took longer, because they analysed the AI's choices and reread the original text. That reshaped deployment and how output was presented. The same pattern showed in the newsroom — senior journalists could already match Fact Genie's speed on some tasks, while junior journalists got much faster and found it easier to meet Reuters' standards. Third, Peters on resistance: adoption caused fear, “some vocal, some through cynicism”, and his conclusion is that new tools may not always fit perfectly, “but resisting change leads to a product that adds no value in the long run”.

Throughout, the framing is trust. Rocha: “We're firmly committed to having the human in the loop to stand by any AI-assisted work.” Prasad's closing takeaway is that journalists must be at the centre of AI workflows, and tools should enhance speed and clarity without disrupting trust in reporting.

Healthcare's moral emergency: reconnecting healthcare with its mission and purpose

Source note: the BMJ page is behind a hard Cloudflare block and the article is subscription-only. This summary is built from the BMJ Group's own press release and a detailed excerpt-based write-up in Health Exec, both of which quote the piece directly. Quotes below are as those sources render them, not checked against the journal page itself.

Published in the BMJ on 20 May 2026 by Don Berwick, Maureen Bisognano and Bob Klaber. Berwick and Bisognano are emeritus presidents of the Institute for Healthcare Improvement in Boston, and Berwick is a former CMS administrator; Klaber is at Imperial College London.

Their argument is that healthcare has accumulated extraordinary technical power while quietly losing the human, moral and relational foundations of care on which its effectiveness ultimately depends. Patients increasingly feel processed rather than cared for, staff report moral distress and loss of meaning, and the workforce is haemorrhaging people at an unsustainable rate. On industrialisation they are pointed: “Industrial healthcare has become a system that offers care for people like you rather than cares for you. Meanwhile clinicians across every system report spending the majority of their time not with patients but grappling with the administrative apparatus surrounding them, their attention and their energy drained before the clinical encounter even begins.”

The organising idea is borrowed from Julia Unwin: two lexicons in public policy, the rational (measurement, efficiency, resource) and the relational (feelings, narrative, human connection). The rational lexicon “has not been wrong, but it has crowded out the relational—and the consequences of that imbalance are now seen and felt everywhere”. Healthcare globally “has lived almost entirely inside the rational lexicon for 30 years. There is an urgent need to re-establish the relational balance.”

Their defence against the charge of sentimentality rests on evidence. They cite research on NHS culture and behaviour finding organisations where staff felt supported and valued had consistently lower patient death rates, and Michael West's line that “looking after patients requires looking after staff”. “The mechanism is not mysterious. Clinicians who have joy in their work give more of themselves.” The conditions for joyful practice — “clarity of purpose, psychological safety, feeling that what matters to you is actually valued” — are “both achievable and measurable”. Kindness, empirically linked to better staff retention, higher teamworking scores and improved patient outcomes, should move from soft adjunct to the business end of delivering high-quality care. The “What matters to you?” movement is their exemplar: “To ask what matters to a person is to see them as a person… It changes the balance of power from ‘I am taking care of you’ to ‘let us work together.’” They also read the evolution from Triple Aim to Quadruple and Quintuple Aims as “a progressive moral recognition”, since population health, patient experience and reduced cost “cannot be achieved with a demoralized workforce—and cannot be achieved equitably without confronting structural disadvantage”.

The practical ask is small-scale and immediate: “Every ward round, every clinical consultation, every leadership conversation is a small but powerful opportunity for all of us to balance relational practice with the rational systems and processes that surround us.” And the close: “We do not need to wait for system reform. We can begin now on our collective leadership challenge to reconnect healthcare with its mission and purpose.”
