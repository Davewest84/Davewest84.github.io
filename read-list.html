Leading Against the Grain – Victor Adebowale

This is the text of a lecture Lord Adebowale gave to the Black Leaders Network in London on the twenty ninth of October, the second of a pair of speeches following his keynote in Leeds earlier in the year. The Leeds speech argued that good leadership is anti-racist leadership, the same thing rather than a separate one. This one asks what it costs to live by that argument, and what should be built so the next generation does not pay the same price in silence.

He opens with HSJ. A few weeks ago, he says, the journal ran a story about a white trust chief executive who called out racism in his own organisation without hedging. His point is that it only became a headline because doing the right thing on race is still against the grain. If it were normal competent leadership, it would have been an all-staff email.

The core of the lecture is what he calls a taxonomy of survival. Early in his career a well-meaning senior figure told him to tone down his intelligence if he wanted to get on in rooms with white people. From that, he names two strategies Black leaders adopt. Compliance means going along with the unwritten rules of institutional racism, including sometimes joining in the undermining of colleagues who make the institution uncomfortable. In his words, "It lets you stay. It lets you rise. It just does not let you lead." The second, which he calls soothsaying and thinks is worse, is actively reassuring white leaders who want to feel good about not knowing very much, and damping down dissent on their behalf. He is explicit that every Black leader, himself included, has done both at some point.

The price of refusing them, he argues, comes in two parts: being coded as a risk rather than a safe pair of hands, and the campaigner label, which treats a Black leader's broader system work as decorative. He notes that despite turning round charities, growing one from nineteen million to a hundred and fifty million pounds and sitting on NHS England's board, he is still read first as a campaigner.

He then sets out four techniques: systemic sight, drawing on James Baldwin; principled governance, arguing that governance is a neutral tool and that racism thrives in a values vacuum; mutual validation, the idea that white leaders get too little critique while Black leaders get too little validation; and treating leadership as a professional practice like clinical work, with something akin to CPD and revalidation. He welcomes NHS England's new Leadership and Management Framework and the government's acceptance of Lord Mann's review, but says the framework should fold anti-racist and system leadership into one curriculum. Of note for HSJ, he says he will not be chair of the NHS Alliance beyond February, and intends to put that time into this work.

The Lenovo Googlebook Duet m is my dream device, and I’m glad it hasn’t launched yet – Chrome Unboxed

Robby Payne writes about the one device he most wanted to see at Google's September launch event in New York: Lenovo's thirteen inch OLED detachable, the Googlebook Duet m. It was tracked for months, reached firmware lock in the spring, then mysteriously didn't appear on stage alongside the five launch clamshells.

Having now spent weeks daily driving those first Googlebooks, he's glad it was held back. His verdict on the new operating system is that it deserves patience as a high ceiling successor to ChromeOS, but that it left far too much of ChromeOS behind out of the box: a Chrome browser missing features, half baked virtual desks and a clunky Files app. On a clamshell those are irritations. On a flagship detachable, combined with a rough handover between desktop and tablet modes, he thinks it would have been a bust, and the conversation would have been about unfinished software rather than the OLED screen, the MediaTek chip, sixteen gigabytes of memory and the clever keyboard that works over both Bluetooth and pogo pins.

He sets out three things that need to be in place. First, parity with Chromebook productivity tools when the keyboard is attached, meaning proper virtual desks, window snapping and a desktop grade file manager. Second, Chrome at full desktop parity, where he says Chrome one five six's fourteen desktop upgrades show real progress. Third, a Pixel Tablet style touch experience the moment you detach the keyboard, rather than a shrunk down desktop fighting your fingers. Get all three right, he argues, and you'd have something no Chromebook Duet, and frankly no iPad Pro, has quite managed.

The keyboard deck has now cleared the FCC, so the hardware is ready. But he thinks rushing it out would be a mistake, and that holding it for a marquee launch at CES in Las Vegas in January makes complete sense, giving Google another two months to fix the software.
