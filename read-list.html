Is welfare spending "out of control"? (Resolution Foundation, Alex Clegg, 25 November 2025)

A note on timing first: this is a comment piece by Alex Clegg, senior economist at the Resolution Foundation, published on 25 November 2025, the day before Rachel Reeves's autumn Budget last year. So it is a year old, but the argument is squarely about the question that is still live now: whether the welfare bill could be cut instead of raising taxes.

Clegg's starting point is that tax rises were coming because the public finances had deteriorated, the government had U-turned on winter fuel payments and on health and disability benefit cuts, and the OBR was expected to downgrade productivity. Opposition parties were arguing the welfare budget should be cut instead, on the grounds that spending had spiralled out of control. The piece tests that claim.

Measured as a share of GDP, total welfare spending in Britain in 2025-26 is estimated at 10.8 per cent. That is 0.8 percentage points higher than on the eve of the financial crisis, but 1.2 points lower than its post-crisis peak in 2012-13, and it is forecast to rise by only 0.1 per cent of GDP out to 2029-30. Clegg argues a social security system that keeps pace with living standards should grow in real terms anyway, and since tax receipts also grow with the economy, constant welfare spending as a share of GDP does not by itself require tax rises.

In real terms the picture is a £44bn rise since the low point of 2019-20, from £280bn to £324bn. He breaks that down. £21bn comes from pensioners, mostly the triple lock and a growing pensioner population. Nobody contests this politically, and scrapping the triple lock from next year would only save around £750m by 2029-30. Meaningful savings there would mean cutting the state pension itself.

The second pressure is non-pensioner disability and incapacity benefits, up £24bn since 2019-20, driven by caseloads: 830,000 more families on incapacity benefits and 1.4 million more working-age adults on disability benefits by May 2025. But, he says, cutting entitlements has never actually delivered savings. The March 2025 cuts to PIP and the universal credit health element were meant to raise £8bn, £6bn of which was reversed. And when the coalition introduced PIP in 2013 it was expected to save £1.4bn by the end of that parliament. It saved 7 per cent of that.

The two figures add up to more than £44bn because everything else, meaning working-age benefits unrelated to health, has fallen by £1bn in real terms since 2019-20, after bigger falls before that. The benefit cap, the two-child limit, the bedroom tax and a four-year freeze meant working-age households on benefits lost an average of £1,500 a year between 2010-11 and 2024-25.

His conclusion is blunt: the standard working-age system has been "cut to the bone since 2013", pensioner spending is politically untouchable, and on health-related benefits "entitlement cuts alone have never been a successful route to savings". Anyone claiming the tax rises could have been avoided by cutting welfare, he says, needs to explain how.

The budget: No rabbits, but a prevention PDEL and good growth for health (perhaps?) (David Buck, LinkedIn, 28 October 2026)

This is a short reaction piece from David Buck of the King's Fund, published on 28 October 2026, the day of this year's Budget. He says there was never going to be a big rabbit-out-of-the-hat moment, but one genuine surprise, to him at least, was the commitment to a prevention departmental expenditure limit, or PDEL.

He credits Demos, the Health Foundation and the Institute for Government, among others, for campaigning for it. The idea is to put prevention on a similar footing to capital spending in government accounts. His case for why it matters is about transparency rather than money. In his words: "if we can't see what government is actually spending or committing to prevention how can we really know whether there is more or less of it, or government is walking the walk not just talking the talk?" He flags that getting to consistent definitions is "a big task in itself". He notes the new Welsh government has made similar commitments in principle, and that Scotland and English health systems are also doing more to identify and measure prevention spend. The first step to doing more prevention, he argues, is understanding how much is being done now.

Beyond the NHS, he picks out budget measures with health effects: levies on gambling, cuts to some taxes on small alcohol producers, more funding to cut rough sleeping, and support on energy costs.

But his bigger long-run point is about fiscal devolution. The framework published alongside the Budget gives more funds and borrowing to mayoral authorities, intended to deliver what the government calls good growth. For Buck, good growth means growth that is equitable and reduces health inequalities, in line with the new duties on mayors under the community empowerment and devolution act. He thinks English devolution could stitch together the wider determinants of health in a way that makes a big dent in avoidable health inequalities, something the prime minister himself alluded to at PMQs just before the Budget speech. And yet, he observes, the fiscal devolution framework does not mention health or prevention once.

He frames the Budget as only the first act of three, ahead of the prime minister's plan for government and the rewiring the state agenda, and calls for more join-up between the Treasury, the Department of Health and Social Care and Number Ten on devolution and health. A prevention PDEL is a step forward, he concludes, but good growth needs a wider purpose, including helping mayors meet their health and inequalities duties.

Worth noting one comment under the post, from Sir Chris Ham: "Surprising the degree of uncritical excitement about PDEL especially when we have no idea yet how it will work."
